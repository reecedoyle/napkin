<SlideLayout title="Non-examples — when 'obvious' constructions aren't functors">

Not every object-to-object recipe is a functor. Two instructive failures:

- **Automorphism group.** Given a group $G$, you can build $\Aut(G)$. But there's no natural way to turn a homomorphism $G_1 \to G_2$ into a map $\Aut(G_1) \to \Aut(G_2)$ — nothing tells you what to do with an automorphism of $G_1$ once $G_2$ is bigger or smaller.
- **Center.** Given a group $G$, you can build its center $Z(G)$ (the elements commuting with everything). Again, this isn't functorial in any natural way — a homomorphism doesn't have to send central elements to central elements.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Why this matters">
When a theorem tells you "$F$ is a functor," read it as "$F$ has a deep hidden symmetry — this is genuinely useful," not as "this is a trivial bookkeeping remark." Functoriality is a real, checkable property that most naive constructions actually *fail*.
</Callout>

One more (subtler) non-example for now: the dual-space operation $V \mapsto V^\vee$ *is* functorial — but not as a covariant functor $\Vect_k \to \Vect_k$. It only becomes a functor once we let arrows flip direction, which is exactly what the next two sections build up to.

</SlideLayout>
