<SlideLayout title="Hom-sets as a bifunctor">

Here's the richest example on the list. Let $\cA$ be any category, and consider pairs of objects $(A_1, A_2)$. We can build the <Term k="fan67HomSet">Hom-set</Term> $\Hom_{\cA}(A_1, A_2)$ — the set of arrows from $A_1$ to $A_2$. This turns out to be a functor
$$
\cA\op \times \cA \to \Set.
$$
Why the $\cA\op$ on the first slot? Given arrows $A_1' \taking{p} A_1$ and $A_2 \taking{q} A_2'$, an arrow $f \in \Hom(A_1,A_2)$ turns into $q \circ f \circ p \in \Hom(A_1', A_2')$. Post-composing with $q$ goes the same direction as $q$ (covariant in the second slot); pre-composing with $p$ goes *against* the direction of $p$ (contravariant in the first slot).

If we "pre-fill" the first slot at a fixed object $A$, we get a functor with only one slot left:
$$
H^A \colon \cA \to \Set, \qquad H^A(A_1) \defeq \Hom_{\cA}(A, A_1).
$$
This is the <Term k="fan67CovariantYoneda">covariant Yoneda functor</Term> — informally, $H^A$ records how $A$ sees the rest of the category. We'll spend all of §7 on why this innocuous-looking functor is such a big deal.
