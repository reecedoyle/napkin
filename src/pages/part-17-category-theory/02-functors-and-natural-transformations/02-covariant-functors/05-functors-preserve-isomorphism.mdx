<SlideLayout title="Functors preserve isomorphism">

Here's why we should care about all this. Consider the "obvious" fact: if groups $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic, then they have the same size. We can restate this using the forgetful functor $U \colon \Grp \to \Set$: if $G \cong H$, then $U(G) \cong U(H)$ (isomorphic sets, i.e. same cardinality). The beauty of category theory is that this works *for any functor whatsoever*, and the proof only uses arrows.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Functors preserve isomorphism">
If $A_1 \cong A_2$ are <Term k="isomorphic">isomorphic</Term> objects in $\cA$ and $F \colon \cA \to \cB$ is a functor, then $F(A_1) \cong F(A_2)$.
</Callout>

  solution="Apply F to both f and g to get F(f) : F(A₁) → F(A₂) and F(g) : F(A₂) → F(A₁). Since g ∘ f = id_{A₁}, functoriality gives F(g) ∘ F(f) = F(g ∘ f) = F(id_{A₁}) = id_{F(A₁)}. Symmetrically F(f) ∘ F(g) = id_{F(A₂)}. So F(g) is a two-sided inverse of F(f), making F(f) an isomorphism — using exactly the two functor axioms, preservation of composition and of identities."
/>

Since almost every operation we perform *is* a functor, and functors always preserve isomorphism, we get isomorphism-invariance for free, all over mathematics. (This is exactly why homotopic spaces have isomorphic fundamental groups — $\pi_1$ is a functor.)
