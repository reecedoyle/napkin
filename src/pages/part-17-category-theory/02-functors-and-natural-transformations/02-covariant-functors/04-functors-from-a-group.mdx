<SlideLayout title="Functors out of a one-object category">

Here's a slick example that recovers something you already know. Let $G$ be a group, and let $\cG = \{\ast\}$ be the <Term k="fan67OneObjectCategory">one-object category</Term> associated to it: one object $\ast$, and arrows $\ast \to \ast$ given by the elements of $G$.

<Callout kind="example" title="Functors from 𝒢 recover group actions">
Consider a functor $F \colon \cG \to \Set$, and let $S = F(\ast)$. Every arrow $g \colon \ast \to \ast$ becomes a function $F(g) \colon S \to S$, and functoriality forces $F(g_1 g_2) = F(g_1) \circ F(g_2)$ and $F(1_G) = \id_S$. That's *exactly* the data of a <Term k="groupAction">group action</Term> of $G$ on $S$.

The same idea with target $\FDVect_k$ instead of $\Set$ recovers a linear representation of $G$: a functor $\cG \to \FDVect_k$ is the same data as embedding $G$ into the invertible $n \times n$ matrices.
</Callout>

So "a group acting on a set" and "a functor out of the one-object category" are two descriptions of the same phenomenon — one emphasizes the action, the other emphasizes that it's a structure-preserving map of categories.
