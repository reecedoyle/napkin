<SlideLayout title="Why the Yoneda lemma isn't as crazy as it looks">

The Yoneda lemma can feel like a magic trick the first time you see it. Here's an unsatisfying-but-honest way to make it feel less crazy.

<Callout kind="remark" title="A rule of thumb">
In category theory, two objects of the same "type" that are built naturally out of the same ingredients are probably secretly the same object. We've already seen this theme: composing functors, composing natural transformations — none of that needed any arbitrary choices.
</Callout>

Now apply the rule of thumb here. We start with a pair of ingredients: an object $A \in \cA$ and a presheaf $X \in [\cA\op,\Set]$. From these two ingredients, we built a *set* of natural transformations $\Hom(H_A, X)$ — but is there another, more obvious way to manufacture a set out of the same two ingredients? Yes: just evaluate, and look at $X(A)$ directly. The Yoneda lemma is telling us our heuristic holds up: these two "obviously constructed" sets from the same raw materials are, in fact, one and the same.

</SlideLayout>
