<SlideLayout title="The universal property of the equalizer">

Now we do to forks exactly what we did to cones over a family of objects: demand a *universal* one.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Equalizer, categorically">
The **equalizer** of $X \taking{f,g} Y$ is an object $E$ together with a map $E \taking{e} X$, satisfying $f \circ e = g \circ e$, such that for every fork $A \taking{q} X$ (with $f \circ q = g \circ q$) there is a **unique** map $A \taking{h} E$ with
$$
e \circ h = q.
$$
</Callout>

In words: every fork through $X$ must factor uniquely through $E$. This is the same shape of statement we saw for products — $E$ is the <Term k="lim68UniversalProperty">**universal**</Term> fork, the one every other fork maps into in exactly one way. And just as with products, an equalizer need not exist in an arbitrary category; but when it does, it is pinned down up to a unique isomorphism, since it's characterized entirely by this universal property rather than by what its elements "are".

</SlideLayout>
