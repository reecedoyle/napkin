<SlideLayout title="Products, category by category">

The whole point of a universal property is that you *don't* need to construct the object explicitly to know it's essentially unique — but concrete descriptions are still good to have on hand.

<Callout kind="example" title="Products, worked out">
- In $\catname{Set}$, the product of $X$ and $Y$ is their Cartesian product $X \times Y$.
- In $\catname{Grp}$, the product of $G$, $H$ is the group direct product $G \times H$.
- In $\catname{Vect}_k$, the product of $V$ and $W$ is the direct sum $V \oplus W$.
- In $\catname{CRing}$, the product of $R$ and $S$ is the ring product $R \times S$.
- In a poset viewed as a category, the product of $x$ and $y$ is the greatest lower bound: $\min\{x, y\}$ in $(\RR, \le)$; $x \cap y$ for subsets ordered by inclusion; $\gcd(x, y)$ for positive integers ordered by division.
</Callout>

Notice how different "the product" looks concretely in each row — Cartesian product, direct sum, greatest common divisor — yet each one satisfies exactly the same <Term k="oam66UniversalProperty">universal property</Term>. That's the payoff of working arrows-only: one definition, many faces.
