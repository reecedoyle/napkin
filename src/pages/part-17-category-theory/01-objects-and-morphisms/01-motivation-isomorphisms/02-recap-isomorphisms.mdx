<SlideLayout title="Recall — isomorphisms everywhere">

Let's recall how "isomorphic" was defined in each setting we've studied so far. In every case, the definition is really about a *pair of mutually inverse maps*, not about the objects' internal contents.

<Callout kind="example" title="Isomorphisms, setting by setting">
- Two groups $G$ and $H$ are <Term k="isomorphic">isomorphic</Term> if there is a bijective <Term k="homomorphism">homomorphism</Term>: equivalently, homomorphisms $\phi \colon G \to H$ and $\psi \colon H \to G$ with $\phi \circ \psi = \id_H$ and $\psi \circ \phi = \id_G$.
- Two metric or <Term k="topologicalSpace">topological spaces</Term> $X$ and $Y$ are isomorphic if there is a <Term k="continuous">continuous</Term> bijection $f \colon X \to Y$ whose inverse $f^{-1}$ is also continuous.
- Two <Term k="vectorSpace">vector spaces</Term> $V$ and $W$ are isomorphic if there is a bijective <Term k="linearMap">linear map</Term> $T \colon V \to W$ — equivalently, $T$ and $T^{-1}$ are both linear.
- Two rings $R$ and $S$ are isomorphic if there is a bijective ring homomorphism $\phi$ — equivalently, two mutually inverse ring homomorphisms.
</Callout>

Notice the pattern repeating: in each bullet, we have some collection of objects, some maps between them, and "isomorphic" means a pair of maps that compose (in both orders) to the identity. Nothing here refers to what the objects are *made of* — only to the maps.
