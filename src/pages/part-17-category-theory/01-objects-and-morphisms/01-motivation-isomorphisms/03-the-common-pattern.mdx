<SlideLayout title="The common pattern">

In each case from the last slide we have:

1. A collection of **objects** (groups, spaces, vector spaces, rings).
2. A collection of **maps** between pairs of objects (homomorphisms, continuous maps, linear maps, ring homomorphisms).
3. A way to **compose** two maps into a new one, and an **identity** map on each object.
4. A notion of **isomorphism**, defined purely in terms of maps: a map with a two-sided inverse.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Moral">
One can learn everything relevant about objects by looking only at the maps between them. If we abstract away *what* the objects and maps actually are, and remember only that maps compose associatively and every object has an identity map, we arrive at the definition of a **category**.
</Callout>

That's exactly what we do next: strip away the specifics of groups, spaces, and rings, and keep only the shared skeleton — objects, arrows, composition, identity.
