<SlideLayout title="Why category theory?">

Over the last several parts we've built up groups, rings, vector spaces, and topological spaces, each with its own flavor of "structure-preserving map" — homomorphisms, linear maps, continuous maps. Category theory is the observation that all of these stories are, at a high enough altitude, *the same story*.

We're not going to try to do category theory full justice here — that's a subject with its own napkin's worth of chapters. Instead this chapter gives a high-level overview of how the concepts you've already met can be recast in categorical language: a single vocabulary of **objects** and **arrows** that applies uniformly to groups, rings, spaces, sets, and posets alike.

<Callout kind="remark" title="A CS-shaped way to think about it">
If you've worked with typed functional programming, this move should feel familiar: instead of asking "what is this value made of?", you ask "what operations can I do with it, and how do they compose?" Category theory does exactly this to the whole of mathematics — it studies objects entirely through the arrows between them.
</Callout>

We'll draw examples from every previous part. Feel free to skim over any example built from a topic you haven't seen yet — the categorical pattern will still make sense.
