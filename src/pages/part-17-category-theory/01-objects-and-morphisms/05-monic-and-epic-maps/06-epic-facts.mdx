Just as with monic, the categories we've spent the most time with are well-behaved: epic and surjective coincide there.

<ProofReveal
  client:load
  id="oam-monep-proof-epic-surjective"
  prompt="Show that in Set, Grp, Ab, Vect_k, Top, the notions of epic and surjective coincide. (Hint for Set: take A = {0, 1}.)"
  solution="Surjective ⟹ epic holds in any concrete category by the dual of the injective ⟹ monic argument: if f is surjective and g∘f = h∘f, then for any y ∈ Y pick x with f(x) = y, so g(y) = g(f(x)) = h(f(x)) = h(y); since y was arbitrary, g = h. For the converse (epic ⟹ surjective) in Set: suppose f: X → Y is epic but not surjective, so some y₀ ∈ Y is missed. Let A = {0, 1} and define g: Y → A to be constant 0, and h: Y → A to send y₀ to 1 and everything else to 0. Then g∘f = h∘f (both constant 0 on X, since f never hits y₀), but g ≠ h — contradicting epic. So f must be surjective. For Ab and Vect_k the same idea works cleanly: quotient Y by the image of f (a subgroup of an abelian group, or a subspace, is automatically a normal subobject) and compare the quotient map Y → Y/im(f) with the zero map — the two agree exactly on im(f) but differ off it. Grp and Top are genuinely subtler and the easy quotient trick does NOT generalize to them: in Grp a subgroup need not be normal, so you cannot just quotient by im(f); in Top a two-point discrete target need not receive a continuous map. Each needs a cleverer construction — for Grp, embed Y into the symmetric group on the cosets Y/im(f) plus one extra point and use two different actions that agree exactly on im(f); for Top, glue two copies of Y along Y minus a missed point (the 'line with two origins'). Both still produce distinct maps out of Y equal on im(f), so epic ⟹ surjective still holds there — it just takes more work than the abelian case."
