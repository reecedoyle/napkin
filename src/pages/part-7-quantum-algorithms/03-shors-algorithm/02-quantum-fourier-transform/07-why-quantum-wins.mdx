<SlideLayout title="Why the quantum version wins">

The key is the tensor product factorization. Classically, computing the DFT requires accessing each of the $N$ amplitudes individually — you genuinely need $\Theta(N)$ operations just to read the input.

Quantum mechanically, the $N = 2^n$ amplitudes $(x_0, \ldots, x_{N-1})$ are all stored simultaneously in the $n$-qubit quantum state. The QFT can be applied by acting locally on individual qubits and pairs of qubits.

<Callout kind="remark" title="The catch">
The QFT does not let you *read out* all $N$ amplitudes. When you measure the output state, you collapse it to a single basis state $\ket{j}$, sampled with probability $|y_j|^2$. The power of the QFT is that when the input has a periodic structure, the output probabilities are concentrated — so a single measurement reveals useful information about the period.
</Callout>

Complexity summary:

| Algorithm | Operations | For $N = 2^n$ |
|-----------|------------|---------------|
| Naive DFT | $O(N^2)$ | $O(4^n)$ |
| Classical FFT | $O(N \log N)$ | $O(n \cdot 2^n)$ |
| **Quantum QFT** | **$O(n^2)$** | **$O(n^2)$** |
