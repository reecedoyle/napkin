<SlideLayout title="Applying the QFT — destructive interference">

Now apply $\UQFT$ to the first register $\ket{\phi} = \ket{k_0} + \ket{k_0+r} + \ket{k_0+2r} + \cdots$.

The coefficient of $\ket{j}$ in $\UQFT\ket{\phi}$ is proportional to

$$\omega_N^{k_0 j} \cdot \left(\omega_N^{0} + \omega_N^{jr} + \omega_N^{2jr} + \omega_N^{3jr} + \cdots\right).$$

This inner sum is a <Term k="geometricSeries">geometric series</Term> of roots of unity. It is nearly zero by <Term k="destructiveInterference">destructive interference</Term> unless $\omega_N^{jr} = 1$, i.e.\ unless $jr/N$ is an integer.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="QFT output concentrates near multiples of N/r">
Approximately,

$$\UQFT(\ket{\phi}) \approx \sum_{\substack{0 \leq j < N \\ jr \equiv 0 \pmod{N}}} \ket{j}$$

up to scaling. So when we measure $\UQFT\ket{\phi}$, we obtain a $j$ with $jr/N$ close to an integer.
</Callout>

In other words: a single measurement of the QFT output gives us a random multiple $j \approx sN/r$ for some integer $s$.

</SlideLayout>
