<Callout kind="definition" title="Good residue mod M">
Call $x \in \Zm{M}$ a **good** residue if all three conditions hold:

1. $\gcd(x, M) = 1$ (so the order of $x$ is defined),
2. the order $r$ of $x \pmod M$ is even, and
3. neither $(x^{r/2} - 1)$ nor $(x^{r/2} + 1)$ is $\equiv 0 \pmod M$.

Under these conditions, $\gcd(x^{r/2} - 1, M)$ equals $p$ or $q$.
</Callout>

The remarkable fact — proved by a Chinese Remainder Theorem argument over the two prime factors $p$ and $q$ — is that at least half of all residues in <Term k="ZmM">$(\mathbb{Z}/M\mathbb{Z})^\times$</Term> are good.

So the algorithm is randomized: pick $x$ uniformly at random, check if $\gcd(x, M) > 1$ (if so, we accidentally found a factor already), otherwise find the order of $x$ and try. We succeed with probability $\geq 1/2$ on each attempt.

Order-finding is the hard part. That is where the quantum computer comes in.

</SlideLayout>
