<Callout kind="remark" title="Why 'inverse'?">
The transform we defined — with the $+jk$ exponent in $\omega_N^{jk}$ — is traditionally called the **inverse** discrete Fourier transform. The "forward" DFT uses $\omega_N^{-jk}$ instead. The naming is a historical accident and causes genuine confusion.

Confusingly, the corresponding quantum operation is called the (not-inverted) quantum Fourier transform, so the signs are swapped again.
</Callout>

Don't let the naming derail you. The important facts are:

- The transform is a linear operation on an $N$-vector of complex numbers.
- It converts between "time domain" (the signal $x$) and "frequency domain" (the amplitudes $y$).
- For a periodic input, the amplitudes peak at predictable frequencies.
- The forward and inverse transforms are just matrix transposes of each other, differing only in the sign of the exponent.

For Shor's algorithm, what matters is that the quantum analogue can be implemented with far fewer operations than the classical one.
