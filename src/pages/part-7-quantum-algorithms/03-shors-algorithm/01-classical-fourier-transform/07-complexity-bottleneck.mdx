<SlideLayout title="The classical complexity bottleneck">

Applying the DIFT naively — multiplying the $N \times N$ matrix by the input vector — takes $O(N^2)$ multiplications.

The <Term k="fft">fast Fourier transform</Term> (FFT) improves this dramatically by reusing intermediate computations. It brings the classical cost down to $O(N \log N)$.

For Shor's algorithm, we need to factor $M = pq$. We'll take $N = 2^n$ where $n \approx 2\log_2 M$. So:

- The naive transform costs $O(N^2) = O(M^4)$ — hopelessly slow.
- The classical FFT costs $O(N \log N) = O(M^2 \log M)$ — much better, but still polynomial in $M$.
- We need $O((\log M)^2) = O(n^2)$ — exponentially better.

<Callout kind="remark" title="The quantum advantage">
The QFT achieves $O(n^2)$ quantum gate operations for an $n$-qubit input, where $N = 2^n$. This is not just a constant-factor improvement — it is exponentially fewer operations than any classical algorithm can achieve.
</Callout>
