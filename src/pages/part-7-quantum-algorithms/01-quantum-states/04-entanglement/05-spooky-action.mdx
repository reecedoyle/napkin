<SlideLayout title="Spooky action at a distance">

The singlet-state phenomenon has a name: Einstein called it <Term k="spukhafteWirkung">spukhafte Fernwirkung</Term> — "spooky action at a distance". Imagine qubit $A$ in a lab in Tokyo and qubit $B$ in London. The moment someone measures $A$ and collapses the singlet state, the state of $B$ is determined — instantly, no matter the distance.

<Callout kind="remark" title="The moral">
States which are not pure tensors in $H_A \otimes H_B$ correspond to **entangled** states. The qubits cannot be described independently; the state of the system must be given as a whole. Entangled states mean the qubits somehow depend on each other, even when separated.
</Callout>

This does **not** allow faster-than-light signalling — the outcome seen in Tokyo is random, so no information is transmitted. But it shows that quantum correlations are stronger than anything classical probability can explain.

Entanglement is the engine behind quantum error correction, quantum key distribution, and — in the next chapter — quantum computation.

</SlideLayout>
