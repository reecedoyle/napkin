The strangest part of quantum measurement is what happens **after** you look.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Quantum collapse">
Suppose we measure $\ket{\psi} = \sum_i c_i\ket{i}_T$ along $T$ and observe eigenvalue $\lambda$.  Let $H_\lambda$ be the eigenspace for $\lambda$ — the span of all $\ket{i}_T$ with $\lambda_i = \lambda$.  After the measurement the state **collapses** to the projection of $\ket{\psi}$ onto $H_\lambda$:
$$\ket{\psi} \;\longrightarrow\; \sum_{\substack{i \,:\, \lambda_i = \lambda}} c_i\ket{i}_T.$$
All components outside $H_\lambda$ are destroyed. This is called <Term k="quantumCollapse">quantum collapse</Term>.
</Callout>

When $\lambda$ is a simple (non-degenerate) eigenvalue, the collapsed state is just $\ket{i}_T$ (up to normalisation) — a single basis vector. The measurement has pinned the state precisely.

When eigenvalues repeat, the collapse projects onto the whole eigenspace; more information survives.
