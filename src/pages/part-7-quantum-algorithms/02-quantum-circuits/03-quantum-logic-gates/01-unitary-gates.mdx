<SlideLayout title="Quantum gates are unitary maps">

In classical circuits, a gate computes a boolean function. In quantum circuits, a gate is a **linear map** — because the state space is a vector space of superpositions.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Unitary map">
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional inner product space. A linear map $U \colon V \to V$ is <Term k="unitaryMap">**unitary**</Term> if any of the following equivalent conditions holds:

- It preserves inner products: $\langle U(x), U(y) \rangle = \langle x, y \rangle$ for all $x, y \in V$.
- Its adjoint is its inverse: $U^\dagger U = I$.
- It preserves norms: $\|U(x)\| = \|x\|$ for all $x \in V$.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="remark">
**Quantum logic gates are unitary matrices.**

Since unitary maps are always bijections, quantum gates are always reversible — which is why the previous section on reversible classical logic wasn't a detour.
</Callout>

There is one more constraint hidden here: probabilities must sum to $1$. Norm-preservation by $U$ is exactly what guarantees this: if $\|\psi\| = 1$ before a gate, then $\|U\psi\| = 1$ after it.

</SlideLayout>
