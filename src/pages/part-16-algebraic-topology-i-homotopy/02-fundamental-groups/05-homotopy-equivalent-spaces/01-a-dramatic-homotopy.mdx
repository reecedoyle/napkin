<SlideLayout title="A dramatic homotopy">

So far, every time we said two things were "homotopic," we meant *path*-homotopic — a deformation that fixes both endpoints. From here on we'll use the fully general notion of <Term k="homotopy">homotopy</Term>: a continuous $F \colon X \times [0,1] \to Y$ with $F(\cdot, 0) = f$ and $F(\cdot, 1) = g$, for arbitrary continuous $f, g \colon X \to Y$ — no endpoints to keep fixed at all.

This general notion of <Term k="homotopic">homotopic</Term> maps can be dramatic:

<Callout kind="example" title="The zero map is homotopic to the identity on ℂ">
The zero function $z \mapsto 0$ and the identity function $z \mapsto z$ are homotopic as maps <Term k="CC">$\mathbb{C}$</Term> $\to \mathbb{C}$. The deformation is
$$
[0,1] \times \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C} \quad \text{by} \quad (s, z) \mapsto sz.
$$
At $s=0$ everything is squashed to $0$; at $s=1$ we recover the identity.
</Callout>

If a map that collapses the entire plane to a point can be continuously deformed into the identity map, then "homotopic" is clearly a much coarser relation than "equal," or even "path-homotopic."

</SlideLayout>
