The prototype wedge sum, and one you should picture immediately whenever you see the notation $X \vee Y$:

<Callout kind="example" title="S¹ ∨ S¹ is a figure eight">
Let $X = S^1$ and $Y = S^1$, with $x_0 \in X$ and $y_0 \in Y$ any points. Then $X \vee Y$ is a "figure eight": two circles fused together at a single point.
</Callout>

Notice this is genuinely different from the disjoint union $S^1 \amalg S^1$ (two separate, disconnected circles) — the wedge sum's single shared point makes $S^1 \vee S^1$ connected, and in fact it becomes the basic example used to study spaces with "more than one independent loop," which is exactly the kind of question the fundamental group (coming up soon) is built to answer.
