<SlideLayout title="Why we need a basis, not just rectangles">

It's not hard to check that we only need to check basis elements of $X$ and $Y$ themselves — that is,
$$
\left\{ U \times V \mid U, V \text{ basis elements for } X, Y \right\}
$$
is *also* a basis for $X \times Y$. But notice we really did need to allow unions of rectangles, rather than declaring only the rectangles $U \times V$ themselves to be "the" open sets.

<Callout kind="remark" title="An open disk is not a rectangle">
In $\RR \times \RR$, an open unit disk had better be an open set — and it is, as a union of infinitely many open rectangles. But the disk itself is *not* of the form $U \times V$ for any open $U, V \subseteq \RR$. So the basis elements are the building blocks, not the complete list of open sets.
</Callout>
