You've also met a completely different-looking topology on $X \times X$ before: the one induced by the <Term k="productMetric">product metric</Term> (say, ordinary Euclidean distance on pairs). These two constructions had better agree, or "product topology" would be a badly-chosen name.

<ProofReveal
  client:load
  id="tc63-prod-proof-basis-matches-metric"
  prompt="Convince yourself the basis {U × V} gives the same topology on X × X as the product metric does. (So this really is the 'right' definition.)"
  solution="Each basis rectangle U × V is a union of metric balls: around any point (x,y) in U × V, since U and V are open in the metric on X, we can find a small enough radius r so that the metric ball of radius r around (x,y) sits inside U × V (take r to be the smaller of the two 'room to spare' distances in each coordinate). Conversely, every metric ball is a union of rectangles U × V, since we can inscribe a small square (a product of two open intervals) inside any metric ball around a point. Since each topology's basis elements are unions of the other's, the two topologies coincide."
/>
