<SlideLayout title="A non-Hausdorff quotient">

All our examples so far collapsed a *closed* subset $A$. If $A$ isn't closed in $X$, the quotient $X/A$ can get genuinely weird — badly enough that it stops being <Term k="hausdorff">Hausdorff</Term>.

<Callout kind="example" title="A weird quotient">
Let $X = \RR$ and $A = (0, 1)$, an *open* interval. The quotient space $X/A$ consists of the points $(-\infty, 0] \cup \{A/A\} \cup [1, \infty)$, where $A/A$ denotes the single point that all of $(0,1)$ got collapsed to.

Here, $0$ and $A/A$ are two distinct points of $X/A$ — but *every* open set containing $0$ also contains $A/A$. There is no way to separate them with disjoint open neighborhoods.
</Callout>

That failure — two distinct points that no pair of disjoint open sets can separate — is precisely the Hausdorff condition failing. This is a useful cautionary example: the quotient construction is powerful, but it doesn't automatically preserve the nice properties of the space you started with.
