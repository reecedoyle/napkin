Once both directions are reversed, the fact that we started with a *square* becomes irrelevant — a solid and a dashed edge can be merged into a single edge, and equivalently we can picture $\RP^2$ as a circle with half its circumference identified with the other half (antipodal points glued together).

Geometrically, $\RP^2$ has two standard interpretations:

<Callout kind="example" title="Two ways to picture RP²">
- **Lines through the origin.** Each point of $\RP^2$ is a line through the origin in $\RR^3$. Identifying each line with a point of $S^2$ (where it pierces the sphere) almost works, except antipodal points of $S^2$ correspond to the *same* line — so $\RP^2$ is "half a sphere," flattened out.
- **Points at infinity.** Take $\RR^2$ and add a "point at infinity" for every direction a line can point. Now any two lines meet: at an ordinary point if they're not parallel, or at their shared point at infinity if they are. All these points at infinity together form the boundary "line at infinity."
</Callout>

<Callout kind="exercise" title="The two pictures agree">
Convince yourself these two formulations are equivalent, by considering the plane $z = 1$ in $\RR^3$ and intersecting each line from the first picture with that plane.
</Callout>
