<SlideLayout title="Complex projective space">

Everything we did with real coordinates has a direct complex analogue.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Complex projective space">
<Term k="tc63CPn">$\CP^n$</Term> is defined like $\RP^n$, with coordinates $(z_0 : \cdots : z_n)$ under scaling — except now the $z_i$ are complex numbers. As before, $\CP^n$ is $\CC^n$ augmented with points at infinity, which form a copy of $\CP^{n-1}$.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" title="Small cases">
- $\CP^0$ is a single point.
- $\CP^1$ is $\CC$ plus a single point at infinity ("complex infinity"): $\CP^1 = \CC \cup \{\infty\}$. Picture taking the complex plane and adding one point that closes up the *entire* boundary at once — the result is the sphere $S^2$. This picture of $\CP^1$, with its natural coordinate system, is called the **Riemann sphere**.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="remark" title="For Euclidean geometers">
While $\RP^2$ is the natural setting for projective geometry, circle inversion is instead usually done in $\CP^1$: an inversion sending generalized circles to generalized circles only needs a *single* point at infinity, which is exactly what $\CP^1$ (rather than $\CP^2$) provides.
</Callout>

Like $\RP^n$, the space $\CP^n$ is a CW complex, built inductively by welding the boundary of $\CC^n$ onto $\CP^{n-1}$. The difference is that $\CC^n \cong \RR^{2n} \cong D^{2n}$ — so the cells we attach are $D^0, D^2, D^4, \dots$, skipping every odd dimension.

<Callout kind="proposition" title="One cell per even dimension">
$\CP^n$ consists of one cell in each *even* dimension, from $0$ up to $2n$.
</Callout>
