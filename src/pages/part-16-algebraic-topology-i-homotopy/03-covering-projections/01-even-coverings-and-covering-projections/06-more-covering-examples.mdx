<SlideLayout title="More covering examples">

Covering projections aren't limited to $\RR \to S^1$. Here are a few more, all built from wrapping one space around another finitely or infinitely many times.

<Callout kind="example" title="A circle covering itself">
The map $S^1 \to S^1$ by $z \mapsto z^3$ is a covering projection. Every point $w \in S^1$ has three cube roots, and a small enough arc around $w$ has a preimage consisting of three disjoint small arcs, one around each cube root, each mapped homeomorphically back onto the arc around $w$.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" title="Covering projections of the punctured plane">
For $\CC \setminus \{0\}$, two more covering projections show up constantly:
- The exponential map $\exp \colon \CC \to \CC \setminus \{0\}$.
- For each $n$, the $n$th power map $z \mapsto z^n$ on $\CC \setminus \{0\}$.
</Callout>

Notice the pattern: $\RR \to S^1$ and $\exp \colon \CC \to \CC \setminus \{0\}$ both have *infinite* fibers (they "unroll" a loop completely), while $z \mapsto z^n$ has *finite* fibers of size $n$ (it wraps a loop around $n$ times without ever fully unrolling it). We'll see in §3 that the fiber size is exactly the index of $p_\sharp(\pi_1 E)$ inside $\pi_1$ of the base: an infinite fiber comes from the trivial subgroup — the simply-connected universal cover $\RR$ — while a finite fiber of size $n$ comes from the index-$n$ subgroup $n\ZZ \subseteq \ZZ = \pi_1(S^1)$. (This "infinite fiber ⟺ simply-connected total space" reading is special to coverings of $S^1$; a finite covering can have a simply-connected total space too, as with $S^2 \to \RP^2$.)
