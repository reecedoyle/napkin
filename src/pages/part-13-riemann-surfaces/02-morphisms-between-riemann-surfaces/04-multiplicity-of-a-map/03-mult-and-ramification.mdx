<SlideLayout title="Multiplicity, ramification, and branch points">

<Callout kind="definition" title="Multiplicity">
The integer $m$ from the previous slide is the <Term k="rs48multiplicityMap">multiplicity of $f$ at $p$</Term>, written $\mult_p(f)$.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" title="Three examples of multiplicity">
- The map $z \mapsto z^{-2}$, extended to $\CC \to \CC_\infty$, has $\mult_0(f) = 2$ — two sheets above $\infty$ collapse to $0$.
- The function $f(z) = (z-1)(z-2)^5$ has $\mult_2(f) = 5$ — more generally, if $p$ is a root of $f$, then $\mult_p(f)$ equals the order of the root.
- The map $z \mapsto z + 1$ has $\mult_p(f) = 1$ everywhere — no sheet-collapsing occurs.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="definition" title="Ramification and branch points">
A point $p \in X$ with $\mult_p(f) > 1$ is called a <Term k="rs48ramificationPoint">ramification point</Term>. Its image $f(p) \in Y$ is called a <Term k="rs48branchPoint">branch point</Term>.
</Callout>

The moral: every nonconstant holomorphic map locally looks like $z \mapsto z^m$ for some $m \geq 1$. When $m = 1$ the map is a local biholomorphism; when $m > 1$ it wraps $m$ local sheets around the image.

</SlideLayout>
