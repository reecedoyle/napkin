Let $X$ be a Riemann surface.

Before the formal definition, note a notational choice from Miranda's book: a <Term k="rs52lineBundleChart">line bundle chart</Term> maps $\pi^{-1}(U) \to \mathbb{C} \times U$ rather than $U \times \mathbb{C}$. So the coordinate reads $(y, x)$ — fiber value first, base point second — instead of the usual graph convention $(x, y)$.

This is a harmless convention, but worth noting so that diagrams in later slides make sense immediately.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Why view X as a curve?">
In this chapter we visualize $X$ by looking only at its real part — treating it as a $1$-dimensional real curve rather than a $2$-dimensional surface. Two reasons:
- Curves embed in $3$-dimensional space, which we can draw.
- For analytic functions, the behavior on the real axis determines behavior everywhere.

The cost: some intuition slips. The circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ and the hyperbola $x^2 = 1 + y^2$ cut out the same shape in $\mathbb{C}^2$, even though their real parts look different.
</Callout>
