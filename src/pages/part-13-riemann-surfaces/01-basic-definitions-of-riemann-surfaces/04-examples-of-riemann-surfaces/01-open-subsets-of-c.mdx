<SlideLayout title="Open subsets of ℂ">

The simplest Riemann surfaces are the ones that need no welding at all.

<Callout kind="example" title="Connected open subsets of ℂ">
Any connected open subset $U \subseteq \CC$ is a Riemann surface. The atlas has exactly **one** chart: the identity map $\phi \colon U \to U \subseteq \CC$, which is trivially analytic.
</Callout>

Examples include:
- The whole complex plane $\CC$
- The open unit disk $\{ z \in \CC \mid \lvert z \rvert < 1 \}$
- The upper half-plane $\{ z \in \CC \mid \operatorname{Im}(z) > 0 \}$
- A punctured plane $\CC \setminus \{0\}$

These are "boring" in the sense that no welding is needed — the space is already a piece of $\CC$. But they are the building blocks. More interesting Riemann surfaces arise when we weld multiple pieces together.

<Callout kind="remark">
Even these "trivial" examples are rich. The open unit disk and the upper half-plane are conformally equivalent (related by a Möbius transformation), so they carry the same complex structure — but neither is conformally equivalent to $\CC$ (Liouville's theorem). This is already a non-trivial fact.
</Callout>

</SlideLayout>
