<SlideLayout title="More complicated L spaces">

There is no reason to restrict poles to $\infty$. We can mix pole and zero constraints at multiple points.

<Callout kind="example" title="L(−1·3 + 4·i + 5·∞)">
The space $L(-1 \cdot 3 + 4 \cdot i + 5 \cdot \infty)$ consists of meromorphic $f \colon \CC_\infty \to \CC$ satisfying:

- $f$ has a **zero of order at least $1$** at $z = 3$;
- $f$ has a **pole of order at most $4$** at $z = i$;
- $f$ has a **pole of order at most $5$** at $z = \infty$.

For instance, $f(z) = (z-3)^3 / (z-i)^2$ belongs to this space, but $f(z) = (z-3)^2 + 1$ does not (no zero at $3$).
</Callout>

This space is also finite-dimensional. The Riemann-Roch theorem tells us its dimension precisely. For now, notice that multiplying every $f \in L(-1 \cdot 3 + 4 \cdot i + 3 \cdot \infty)$ by $(z-i)^4$ gives a bijection to $L(-1 \cdot 3 + 7 \cdot \infty)$, which consists of polynomials of degree $\leq 7$ with a zero at $3$ — a $7$-dimensional space.
