<SlideLayout title="Principal divisors have degree zero">

A divisor $D$ is called <Term k="rs51principalDivisorDef">principal</Term> if $D = \Div(f)$ for some meromorphic function $f$. The analogy with **principal ideals** in ring theory is precise: just as a principal ideal is generated by one element, a principal divisor is "generated" by one meromorphic function.

<Callout kind="proposition" title="Principal divisors have degree 0">
If $D$ is a principal divisor on a compact Riemann surface $X$, then $\deg D = 0$.
</Callout>

Intuitively: on a compact surface, zeros and poles of a meromorphic function must balance. Every zero of $f$ contributes $+1$ to the degree for each order of vanishing, and every pole contributes $-1$ — and they cancel exactly.

<Callout kind="example" title="Why −1·∞ is not principal">
The divisor $D = (-1) \cdot \infty$ on $\CC_\infty$ has degree $-1 \neq 0$. So $D$ cannot be $\Div(f)$ for any meromorphic $f$ on $\CC_\infty$. There is no meromorphic function with exactly one simple pole at $\infty$ and no zeros — that would require a degree-$-1$ polynomial, which doesn't exist.
</Callout>

</SlideLayout>
