<SlideLayout title="Holomorphic 1-forms — definition">

We have seen that $dz$ and $2z\,dz$ share a special rotation property: moving the input direction by $90°$ counterclockwise multiplies the output by $i$. This is captured by the following definition.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Holomorphic 1-form">
A $1$-form $\omega$ on an open set $U \subseteq \CC$ is <Term k="rs50holomorphicForm">holomorphic</Term> if and only if it can be written as
$$\omega = f(z)\,dz$$
for some holomorphic function $f : U \to \CC$.
</Callout>

Intuitively: $\omega$ is holomorphic exactly when the coefficient of $dz$ is a holomorphic function. The form "inherits" its analytic quality from $f$.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Why holomorphic?">
A holomorphic function $f$ has the property that its total derivative is complex-linear (not just real-linear): $D_p f(v) = f'(p) \cdot v$. This means $f$ scales all directions equally, so $df = f'(z)\,dz$ is well-defined. Non-holomorphic smooth functions distinguish real from imaginary directions, producing forms that aren't pure multiples of $dz$.
</Callout>
