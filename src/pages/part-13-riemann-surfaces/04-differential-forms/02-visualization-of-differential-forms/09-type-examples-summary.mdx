<SlideLayout title="Summary — types and holomorphicity">

Let us collect the four examples from the visualization section.

| Form | Type | Holomorphic? |
|------|------|--------------|
| $dz$ | $(1,0)$ | Yes |
| $2z\,dz$ | $(1,0)$ | Yes |
| $\ol{z}\,dz$ | $(1,0)$ | No ($\ol{z}$ is not holomorphic) |
| $d\ol{z}$ | $(0,1)$ | No |

<Callout kind="remark" title="The hierarchy">
The inclusion goes: **holomorphic** $\subset$ **type $(1,0)$** $\subset$ **smooth $1$-forms**.

A <Term k="rs50holomorphicForm">holomorphic form</Term> $f(z)\,dz$ has a holomorphic coefficient. A <Term k="rs50type10">type $(1,0)$ form</Term> $f(z)\,dz$ allows any smooth coefficient $f$. A general smooth form can mix $dz$ and $d\ol{z}$ parts.
</Callout>

The type decomposition $(1,0)$ vs $(0,1)$ is the first step toward understanding the Dolbeault complex on Riemann surfaces — the appropriate generalization of the exterior derivative that respects complex structure.

</SlideLayout>
