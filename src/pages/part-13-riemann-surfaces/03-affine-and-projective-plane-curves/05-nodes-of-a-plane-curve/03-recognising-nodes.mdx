<SlideLayout title="Recognising nodes algebraically">

How do we recognise a <Term k="rs49node">node</Term> from the polynomial? Expand $f$ in a Taylor series around the singular point $p = (0,0)$ (translate if needed):
$$f(x,y) = Q(x,y) + \text{higher-order terms}$$
where $Q$ is the lowest-degree nonzero homogeneous part. At a <Term k="rs49singularPoint">singular point</Term>, $f(0,0) = 0$ and both first partials vanish, so $Q$ has degree $\geq 2$.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Node criterion">
A singular point $p$ of $\{f = 0\}$ is a **node** if $Q$ — the degree-2 part of $f$ at $p$ — factors into two distinct linear factors over $\mathbb{C}$:
$$Q(x,y) = \lambda_1(x,y) \cdot \lambda_2(x,y), \quad \lambda_1 \not\parallel \lambda_2.$$
The two linear factors give the two tangent directions of the branches.
</Callout>

**For $f = x^2 - y^2$:** The degree-2 part is $Q = x^2 - y^2 = (x-y)(x+y)$. Two distinct factors. Node confirmed.

**For $f = y^2 - x^3$ (a cusp at origin):** The lowest-degree part is $y^2$ — a perfect square, giving only one tangent direction. Not a node: it is a cusp.
