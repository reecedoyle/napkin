<SlideLayout title="Curves as zero sets of polynomials">

We want a uniform way to describe plane curves. The explicit form — "$y = f(x)$" — is clumsy: it requires choosing which variable to solve for, and breaks at vertical tangents.

The better approach is **implicit**: describe the curve as the zero set of a polynomial.

- The line $2x - y = 0$ is the set $\{(x,y) : 2x - y = 0\}$.
- The unit circle is the set $\{(x,y) : x^2 + y^2 - 1 = 0\}$.
- The parabola is $\{(x,y) : y - x^2 = 0\}$.

<Callout kind="remark" title="The key idea">
Many interesting curves in $\mathbb{R}^2$ — and in $\mathbb{C}^2$ — can be written as the set of roots of a polynomial $f(x,y) = 0$.
</Callout>

This generalises cleanly to $\mathbb{C}^2$: replace $\mathbb{R}$ with $\mathbb{C}$ everywhere. The zero set of a polynomial $f(z,w) \in \mathbb{C}[z,w]$ sits inside $\mathbb{C}^2$, but it has complex dimension 1 — that is, one complex degree of freedom — making it a natural candidate for a Riemann surface.

</SlideLayout>
