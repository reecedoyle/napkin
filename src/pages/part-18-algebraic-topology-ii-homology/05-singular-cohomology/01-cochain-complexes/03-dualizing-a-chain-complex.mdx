<SlideLayout title="Dualizing a chain complex">

Aside from de Rham's complex (next slide), **the most common way to produce a cochain complex is to dualize a chain complex.**

Pick an abelian group $G$. Recall $\Hom(-, G)$ is a *contravariant* functor: it turns a map $f \colon A \to B$ into a map $f^\vee \colon \Hom(B,G) \to \Hom(A,G)$ going the other way. Applying it to every group and every map in a chain complex
$$
\dots \taking\partial A_{n+1} \taking\partial A_n \taking\partial A_{n-1} \taking\partial \dots
$$
flips all the arrows, turning it into a cochain complex
$$
\dots \taking\delta A^{n-1} \taking\delta A^n \taking\delta A^{n+1} \taking\delta \dots
$$
where $A^n \defeq \Hom(A_n, G)$, and $\delta$ is defined by $\delta(A_n \taking{f} G) = A_{n+1} \taking\partial A_n \taking{f} G$ — in words, $\delta f = f \circ \partial$.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Why Hom(−,G) is the natural dualizing functor">
This construction, <Term k="sc74HomDualFunctor">$\Hom(-,G)$</Term>, is exactly what lets us go from "chain complex" language (built from geometric simplices) to "cochain complex" language (built from functions on those simplices) without inventing anything new — every step is forced once you fix $G$.
</Callout>

Because these are the cohomology groups we'll study almost exclusively, they get their own notation.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Cohomology with coefficients in G">
Given a chain complex $A_\bullet$ of abelian groups and another abelian group $G$, we let <Term k="sc74HnAG">$H^n(A_\bullet; G)$</Term> denote the cohomology groups of the dual cochain complex $A^\bullet$ obtained by applying $\Hom(-,G)$. In other words, $H^n(A_\bullet; G) = H^n(A^\bullet)$.
</Callout>
