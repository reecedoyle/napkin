<SlideLayout title="The cross product on chains">

The most natural construction: a $m$-dimensional cube in $X$ and an $n$-dimensional cube in $Y$ multiply to an $(m+n)$-dimensional cube in $X \times Y$. The <Term k="aoc75CrossProductChain">cross product</Term> $f \times g$ of an $m$-simplex $f \colon \Delta^m \to X$ and an $n$-simplex $g \colon \Delta^n \to Y$ formalizes this, producing an $(m+n)$-chain $f \times g \in C_{m+n}(X\times Y)$.

The catch: $\Delta^m \times \Delta^n$ isn't itself a simplex, so we have to subdivide it into finitely many copies of $\Delta^{m+n}$ first. This subdivision gets large fast — covering $\Delta^{15}\times\Delta^{15}$ takes $\binom{30}{15} = 155{,}117{,}520$ simplices. (The symbol $\times$ here is unrelated to the vector cross product from $\RR^3$ — it's just shaped like a cross.)

Formally, this gives a function on chains, $C_m(X) \times C_n(Y) \xrightarrow{\times} C_{m+n}(X\times Y)$, and one can show it induces a map on homology:
$$
H_m(X) \times H_n(Y) \xrightarrow{\times} H_{m+n}(X\times Y).
$$
