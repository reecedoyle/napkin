<SlideLayout title="A warning — cup ≠ wedge, cochain-by-cochain">

You can interpret an $n$-form as an $n$-cochain in the obvious way (integrate it over each simplex). It's tempting to guess the cup product is just the wedge product in disguise. It is *not* — not at the cochain level, anyway.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Warning">
Let $X = \RR^2$ and take $dx, dy$ as $1$-cochains. Evaluate $dx \smile dy$ on the triangle $[v_0, v_1, v_2]$ versus on $[v_2, v_1, v_0]$ (same triangle, vertices relisted), where $v_0 = (1,0)$, $v_1 = (0,0)$, $v_2 = (0,1)$ with straight edges. The wedge product $dx \wedge dy$ is alternating, so reversing the vertex list must negate the answer. But the cup product formula has no built-in alternation operator — nothing forces $dx \smile dy$ to flip sign the same way, and indeed it doesn't match $-(dx \smile dy)$ on the reversed list the way $dx \wedge dy$ would.
</Callout>

The saving grace: the discrepancies are themselves *coboundaries*, so they vanish once you pass to cohomology classes. In $H^\bullet(M;\RR)$, the class of $dx \smile dy$ agrees with the class corresponding to $dx \wedge dy$ — this is exactly what the isomorphism $H^\bullet(M;\RR) \cong \HdR^\bullet(M)$ (stated a couple slides ago) is telling us. On the torus, for instance, the $1$-cocycles "$dx$" and "$dy$" cup together to give (up to sign) the generator of $H^2(\text{torus})$, matching $(dx \wedge dy)(T) = \pm 1$ on the whole torus $T$.

So going forward: we care about the cup product on cohomology *classes*, not the finicky cochain-level formula.

</SlideLayout>
