Let's see $\alpha \smile \alpha \neq 0$ concretely on $\RP^2$: the real projective plane is a disk with antipodal points on its boundary circle glued together (equivalently, a $2$-sphere with antipodal points identified). The formula above gives $H^\bullet(\RP^2;\Zc2) \cong \Zc2[\alpha]/(\alpha^3)$: a single nonzero element in each of $H^0$, $H^1$, $H^2$, and nothing above that.

There's a $1$-cycle on $\RP^2$ that isn't homologous to $0$ — informally, a loop that can't be contracted or filled in without leaving $\RP^2$. Every such loop turns out to be homologous to every other one, and running around it *twice* is homologous to $0$. So $H_1(\RP^2;\Zc2) \cong \Zc2$, and the nonzero class $\alpha \in H^1(\RP^2;\Zc2) \cong \Hom(H_1(\RP^2),\Zc2)$ is the function sending that loop to $1$.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Why α ⌣ α is nonzero">
Picture $\RP^2$ as a square with **both** pairs of opposite edges identified with a reversal (the antipodal gluing, boundary word $abab$ — not the Klein-bottle gluing, which reverses only one pair). The class $\alpha$ acts like *both* "$dx$" and "$dy$" simultaneously — both boundary directions get assigned the value $1$. So $\alpha \smile \alpha$ assigns $1$ to the whole surface, exactly the way $dx \smile dy$ assigned a nonzero value to the whole torus. Since it's nonzero on the fundamental class, $\alpha \smile \alpha \neq 0$.
</Callout>
