<SlideLayout title="Specializing to real coefficients">

We now reveal a surprising connection: singular cohomology, with real coefficients, is secretly built from **differential forms**.

Let $M$ be a smooth manifold and specialize the universal coefficient theorem to $G = \RR$, the additive group of real numbers. The universal coefficient theorem writes $H^k(M;\RR)$ in terms of $\Hom(H_k(M), \RR)$ and an $\Ext$ term; the $\Ext$ term vanishes here, so we're left with a clean statement.

<ProofReveal
  client:load
  id="aoc75-drc-proof-ext-vanishes"
  prompt="Check that Ext(H, ℝ) = 0 for any finitely generated abelian group H."
  solution="Write H ≅ ℤ^r ⊕ ℤ/n₁ℤ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ ℤ/nₖℤ. Ext is additive over direct sums, so it suffices to check each summand. Ext(ℤ, ℝ) = 0 since ℤ is free (Ext vanishes against free modules — there's nothing to obstruct, every extension splits). And Ext(ℤ/nℤ, ℝ) = 0 because ℝ is divisible: the obstruction group Ext(ℤ/nℤ, G) is computed as G/nG, and multiplication by n is surjective on ℝ (every real number is n times another), so ℝ/nℝ = 0. Hence Ext(H, ℝ) = 0 for every finitely generated abelian group H."
/>

Because that $\Ext$ term is always zero, the universal coefficient theorem simplifies to a genuine isomorphism (not just an unnatural exact sequence):
$$
H^k(M; \RR) \cong \Hom(H_k(M), \RR) = \left( H_k(M) \right)^\vee
$$
where we view <Term k="homSpace">$H_k(M)$</Term> as a real vector space. So getting a handle on either $H_k(M)$ or $H^k(M;\RR)$ gets us the other for free.
