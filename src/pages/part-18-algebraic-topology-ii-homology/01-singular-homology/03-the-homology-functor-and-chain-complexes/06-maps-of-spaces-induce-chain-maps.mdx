<SlideLayout title="Maps of spaces induce chain maps">

Let's fill in the geometric side, starting with step (1) from the previous slide.

<Callout kind="lemma" title="A map of spaces gives a map of singular chain complexes">
Each map $f \colon X \to Y$ induces a map $C_n(X) \to C_n(Y)$.
</Callout>

The construction is immediate: given a singular $n$-simplex $\sigma \colon \Delta^n \to X$, post-compose to get
$$
\Delta^n \taking{\sigma} X \taking{f} Y,
$$
a singular $n$-simplex in $Y$. Extend linearly to chains. Concretely: a path in $X$ becomes a path in $Y$ (just push it through $f$), a $2$-simplex in $X$ becomes a $2$-simplex in $Y$, and so on. It's routine to check the squares involving $\partial$ commute — $f$ acting on the boundary of $\sigma$ is the same as taking the boundary of $f \circ \sigma$, since both are computed by deleting vertices and then applying $f$.

So $f$ gives an honest chain map $C_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(Y)$, hence (by the algebra we just did) an induced map $f_\ast \colon H_n(X) \to H_n(Y)$. That handles step (1). Step (2) — showing $f_\ast$ only depends on the homotopy class of $f$ — needs the *chain homotopy* machinery, and that's where the real geometry comes in.

</SlideLayout>
