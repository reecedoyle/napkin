<SlideLayout title="Homology of the spheres">

Consider $S^2$, the ordinary $2$-dimensional sphere.

<Callout kind="example" title="Homology groups of S²">
Since $S^2$ is path connected, $H_0(S^2) \cong \ZZ$. We also have $H_1(S^2) = 0$, for the same reason $\pi_1(S^2)$ is trivial. But
$$
H_2(S^2) \cong \ZZ.
$$
Elements of $H_2(S^2)$ correspond to wrapping $S^2$ in a "tetrahedral bag" — once, twice, three times, and so on. So $H_2$ detects the spherical cavity of $S^2$, a hole that $\pi_2$ also detects, but (unlike $\pi_2$) $H_2$ detects *other* kinds of cavities too, not just spherical ones.
</Callout>

More generally, it turns out that for the $m$-dimensional sphere $S^m$:

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Homology of spheres">
$$
H_n(S^m) \cong
\begin{cases}
\ZZ & n = m \text{ or } n = 0 \\
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
$$
</Callout>

One $\ZZ$ for "the space is path-connected" ($n=0$), and one more $\ZZ$ exactly in the sphere's own dimension ($n=m$), detecting the single $m$-dimensional cavity that $S^m$ bounds. Every other homology group vanishes.

</SlideLayout>
