<SlideLayout title="An explicit boundary in the circle">

Here's a fully worked example showing $H_1(S^1)$ arithmetic in action. Let $X = S^1$, let $a$ be the uppermost point and $b$ the lowermost point. Let $c$ be the $1$-simplex from $a$ to $b$ along the left half of the circle, and $d$ the $1$-simplex from $a$ to $b$ along the right half. Let $\gamma$ be the $1$-simplex representing a full loop from $a$ back to $a$, going counterclockwise once around $S^1$.

<Callout kind="example" title="γ = c − d in H₁(S¹)">
We claim that in $H_1(S^1)$, $\gamma = c - d$ — geometrically, $c - d$ represents wrapping once around the circle, exactly what $\gamma$ does.

The reason: there's a $2$-simplex (the whole disk bounded by $S^1$, thought of as a degenerate triangle with $v_0 = v_1 = a$ and $v_2 = b$) whose boundary is exactly $\gamma - c + d$. Since it's the boundary of a $2$-chain, $\gamma - c + d$ is $0$ in $H_1(S^1)$ — so $\gamma = c - d$.
</Callout>

This is why singular homology has "singular" in the name: the $2$-simplex here is embedded in $S^1$ itself (a 1-dimensional space!), which is about as degenerate — as "singular" — as a map can get while still being perfectly legal.
