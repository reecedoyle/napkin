<SlideLayout title="Computing some boundaries">

Let's compute $\partial$ on the three simplices $\sigma^0, \sigma^1, \sigma^2$ from before.

<Callout kind="example" title="Boundary operator">
- $\partial \sigma^0 = 0$ — points have no boundary, by convention.
- $\partial(\sigma^1) = [v_1] - [v_0]$: the "difference" of the endpoint and the start point, as $0$-chains.
- $\partial(\sigma^2) = [v_0,v_1] - [v_0,v_2] + [v_1,v_2]$: the sum of the three oriented edges making up the sides of the triangle.
</Callout>

Notice what happens if we take the boundary *again*, starting from $\sigma^2$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial(\partial(\sigma^2))
&= \partial([v_0,v_1]) - \partial([v_0,v_2]) + \partial([v_1,v_2]) \\
&= \left( [v_1]-[v_0] \right) - \left( [v_2]-[v_0] \right) + \left( [v_2]-[v_1] \right) \\
&= 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Every vertex-difference cancels exactly once against another — $[v_0]$, $[v_1]$, $[v_2]$ each appear once with a $+$ and once with a $-$. This is not a coincidence: it's a general phenomenon we pin down next.
