<SlideLayout title="The Mayer–Vietoris short exact sequence">

Here's the example that drives this whole chapter. Let $X = U \cup V$ be an <Term k="openCover">open cover</Term> of a space $X$ by two open sets.

For each $n$, consider the chains supported in $U$, in $V$, and in $U \cap V$:

$$
c \in C_n(U \cap V) \;\longmapsto\; (c, -c) \in C_n(U) \oplus C_n(V)
$$

$$
(c, d) \in C_n(U) \oplus C_n(V) \;\longmapsto\; c + d \in C_n(U+V)
$$

where <Term k="tle71UPlusV">$C_n(U+V)$</Term> is the subgroup of $C_n(X)$ generated by simplices lying entirely in $U$ or entirely in $V$. A quick check on a basis shows the kernel of the second map is exactly the image of the first.

<Callout kind="example" title="Mayer–Vietoris short exact sequence">
For $X = U \cup V$ an open cover, there is a <Term k="tle71MVShortExact">short exact sequence of chain complexes</Term>
$$
0 \to C_\bullet(U \cap V) \injto C_\bullet(U) \oplus C_\bullet(V) \surjto C_\bullet(U+V) \to 0.
$$
</Callout>

Intuitively: a chain in $U \cap V$ can be recorded from either side, with opposite signs, so it dies in the sum $C_n(U) \oplus C_n(V)$ once you add the two copies back together — that's the injective map. Anything left over after that cancellation is a chain "subordinate to the cover," which is exactly $C_n(U+V)$ — that's the surjective map.

</SlideLayout>
