<SlideLayout title="What goes wrong for p = 0.4?">

The algebraic trick gives $x = 5$ when $p = 0.6$. The same method applied to $p = 0.4$ (downward bias $40\%$) gives $x = -5$ — clearly nonsense.

<Callout kind="remark" title="The hidden flaw">
The derivation assumed the expected hitting time $x$ is **finite**. When $p = 0.4$, there is a nonzero probability that the walk drifts upward forever and never reaches $0$. Then $\EE[\text{hitting time}] = \infty$, and we were subtracting $\infty - \infty$ — an undefined quantity.
</Callout>

(Preview: martingale theory in the next chapter will show that the probability of never reaching $0$ is exactly $1 - \frac{0.4}{0.6} = \tfrac{1}{3}$ when $p = 0.4$.)

This motivates the SLLN: to conclude that the walk *does* hit $0$ in **almost every world** when $p = 0.6$, we need a theorem about almost sure behaviour. That theorem is the strong law of large numbers.

</SlideLayout>
