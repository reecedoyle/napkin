Almost sure convergence is stricter than it looks. Here is the canonical trap.

<Callout kind="example" title="Non-example of almost sure convergence">
An immortal skeleton archer practices shots. On shot $n$ he scores a bulls-eye with probability $1 - \tfrac{1}{n}$ (improving over time). Let $X_n \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 10\}$ be the score of shot $n$.

Although $\Pr[\text{bulls-eye on shot } n] \to 1$, we still have
$$\mu\!\left(\omega \in \Omega : \lim_n X_n(\omega) = 10\right) = 0.$$
</Callout>

Why? On any fixed shot $n$ the archer misses with probability $\tfrac{1}{n}$. By the <Term k="llBorelCantelli">Borel–Cantelli lemma</Term>, since $\sum_{n=1}^\infty \tfrac{1}{n}$ diverges, in almost every world the archer misses infinitely often — so the scores never settle to $10$.

The lesson: "likely on each step" is far weaker than "converges in almost every world."
