<Callout kind="definition" title="σ-algebra generated by X">
Let $X \colon \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ be a random variable. The **sub-$\sigma$-algebra generated by $X$** is

$$\sigma(X) \defeq \{ X^{-1}(B) \mid B \subseteq \mathbb{R} \text{ measurable} \}.$$

If $X_1, X_2, \dots$ is a sequence of random variables, $\sigma(X_1, X_2, \dots)$ is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing $\sigma(X_i)$ for each $i$.
</Callout>

The informal reading: $\sigma(X)$ is precisely the information that knowing $X(\omega)$ gives you. If you know $X(\omega)$, you can determine membership in any set of the form $\{X \in B\}$ — and that's exactly the collection $\sigma(X)$.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Measurability and information">
A random variable $Y$ is $\mathcal{F}$-measurable if and only if $\sigma(Y) \subseteq \mathcal{F}$. Informally: $Y$ is determined by the information in $\mathcal{F}$. You can know the value of $Y$ without needing any information beyond what $\mathcal{F}$ provides.
</Callout>
