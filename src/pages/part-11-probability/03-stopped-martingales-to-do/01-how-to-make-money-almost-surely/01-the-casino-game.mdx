<SlideLayout title="A game that looks unwinnable">

We now take our newfound knowledge of measure theory to a casino.

Here's the setup: a casino lets you bet any amount $n$ on a fair coin flip, but with bad odds — you win $n$ dollars if the coin shows heads, but lose $2n$ dollars if it shows tails. The expected value of each bet is

$$\frac{1}{2}(n) + \frac{1}{2}(-2n) = -\frac{n}{2}.$$

This is clearly a losing game on any single bet. And yet — with unbounded time and money — you can almost surely come out ahead. This seems paradoxical. The chapter unravels exactly why, and what "sane" conditions prevent you from exploiting it.

<Callout kind="example" title="The doubling strategy">
Start by betting $1$ dollar. If you lose, bet $10$ dollars. If you lose again, bet $100$ dollars. Continue multiplying by $10$ each time you lose.

Since a fair coin will eventually land heads (with probability 1), you will eventually win. When you do, the winnings at that step overwhelm all previous losses — you leave with a profit.

The expected number of flips until the first heads is just 2. So why can't you do this in practice?
</Callout>
