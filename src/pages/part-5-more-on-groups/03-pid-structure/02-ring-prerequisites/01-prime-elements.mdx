<SlideLayout title="Ring prerequisites — prime elements in a UFD">

The structure theorem will work for any <Term k="pid">PID</Term> $R$. Before stating it, we need a few facts about UFDs (recall every PID is a UFD).

The intuition carries directly from $R = \ZZ$:

<Callout kind="definition" title="Prime element">
If $R$ is a <Term k="ufd">UFD</Term>, a nonzero element $p \in R$ is a <Term k="primeElement">**prime element**</Term> if the ideal $(p)$ is a <Term k="primeIdeal">prime ideal</Term>. Equivalently: $p$ is not a unit, and whenever $p = xy$ then $x$ or $y$ is a unit.
</Callout>

In $\ZZ$ the prime elements are $\{\pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 5, \dots\}$ — the usual primes and their negatives. In a general UFD every element factors into prime elements, just as in $\ZZ$:
$$r = u \, p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_m^{e_m}$$
where $u$ is a unit and the $p_i$ are prime elements.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Divisibility">
We say $r$ <Term k="divides">**divides**</Term> $s$ (written $r \mid s$) if $s = r'r$ for some $r' \in R$.
</Callout>

In terms of the prime factorisation: $r \mid s$ exactly when the exponent of each prime in $r$ is at most the corresponding exponent in $s$.

</SlideLayout>
