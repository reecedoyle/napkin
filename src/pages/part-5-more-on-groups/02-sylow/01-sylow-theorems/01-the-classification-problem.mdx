<SlideLayout title="The classification problem">

Suppose someone hands you a positive integer $n$ and asks: list every group of order $n$, up to isomorphism.

This is the "find all groups" problem. It sounds simple. It is not.

For small $n$ the answer is short — there are few groups of order $6$ or $10$. But even asking which groups have a given order is deep and subtle. The Sylow theorems are the main tool for attacking it.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Reformulation">
We are looking for all functions $f \colon \{1,\dots,n\} \times \{1,\dots,n\} \to \{1,\dots,n\}$ satisfying the group axioms: identity, associativity, and inverses. The Sylow theorems constrain which orders are even possible for certain subgroup structures.
</Callout>

The key idea: if the order of $G$ has prime-power factors, those factors force certain subgroups to exist — and the number of such subgroups is tightly constrained by arithmetic.
