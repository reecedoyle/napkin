$x^3-2$ breaks the pattern from last slide.

<Callout kind="example" title="Splitting field of x³−2">
The splitting field of $x^3-2$ over $\QQ$ is $\QQ(\cbrt2, \omega)$ — **not** just $\QQ(\cbrt2)$! You genuinely have to adjoin all three roots; they don't generate each other.

- For $x^2-5$: adjoining $\sqrt5$ automatically includes $-\sqrt5$.
- For $x^2+x+1$: adjoining $\omega$ automatically includes $\omega^2$.
- For $x^3-2$: adjoining $\cbrt2$ does **not** give you $\omega\cbrt2$ or $\omega^2\cbrt2$ for free — after all, $\QQ(\cbrt2) \subset \RR$, and those two conjugates aren't real!
</Callout>

So the splitting field of $x^3-2$ over $\QQ$ has degree **six**, not three. In general, the splitting field of a degree-$n$ polynomial with no special relations among its roots can have degree as large as $n!$ — any permutation of the roots is achievable.
