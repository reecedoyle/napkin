Now try $K = \QQ(\cbrt2)$, and something new happens.

<Callout kind="example" title="Embeddings of ℚ(∛2)">
There are **three** embeddings of $K = \QQ(\cbrt2)$ into $\CC$, one for each root of $x^3-2$:

- The identity: $1 \mapsto 1$, $\cbrt2 \mapsto \cbrt2$.
- $1 \mapsto 1$, $\cbrt2 \mapsto \omega\cbrt2$, where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$ is a primitive cube root of unity. (This one choice determines the whole map.)
- $1 \mapsto 1$, $\cbrt2 \mapsto \omega^2\cbrt2$.
</Callout>

Here's the twist: $\QQ(\cbrt2) \subset \RR$ — it's a subfield of the real numbers! But two of its three embeddings send $\cbrt2$ to a genuinely *complex* number, $\omega\cbrt2$ or $\omega^2\cbrt2$. Those embeddings can't possibly land back inside $\QQ(\cbrt2) \subset \RR$; their image has to leave the field entirely.

This is the opposite of what happened with $\QQ(\sqrt2)$ a moment ago, where every embedding's image stayed inside $\QQ(\sqrt2)$.
