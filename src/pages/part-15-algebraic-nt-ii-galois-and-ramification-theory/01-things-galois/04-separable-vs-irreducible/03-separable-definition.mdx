<SlideLayout title="Separable polynomials">

Let's give a name to the property the derivative trick was trying to establish.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Separable">
A polynomial with no double roots is called <Term k="gal58Separable">separable</Term>. We want irreducible polynomials to always be separable — that's what made the "$n$ embeddings" proof in §3 work.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="lemma" title="Separability in characteristic zero">
Any irreducible polynomial over a characteristic-zero field is separable.
</Callout>

This is exactly the derivative argument from before, and it works whenever the field has characteristic $0$ (so a nonconstant polynomial's derivative is never identically zero). It's false in general once you drop that hypothesis — our $\FF_3$ example was a warning sign, though it doesn't immediately produce an inseparable *irreducible* polynomial by itself.

<Callout kind="remark">
Why "separable"? Picture breaking a polynomial into irreducible factors. Normally a double root signals *reducibility* — the factor appears twice. In characteristic $p > 0$ this can fail: you can have a double root that genuinely can't be teased apart into two separate irreducible factors. Inseparable polynomials are the strange case where roots refuse to separate.
</Callout>
