<SlideLayout title="Why F× is cyclic">

Let $F$ be a finite field of order $q$, a prime power. We use the same "at most $d$ roots" idea from earlier in the chapter, one more time.

<Callout kind="question" title="Key counting fact">
For any positive $d < |F^\times| = q - 1$, there are at most $d$ elements $x \in F^\times$ with $x^d = 1$.
</Callout>

<MCQ
  client:load
  id="ff59-s5-mcq-why-at-most-d-roots"
  prompt="Why can a finite field F have at most d elements x with x^d = 1?"

  correct="b"
  explanation="This is the general fact (used earlier for the separability of x^{p^n} − x): a nonzero polynomial of degree d over a field has at most d roots in that field, since each root contributes a distinct linear factor."
/>

Now, $F^\times$ is a <Term k="fgAbelian">finitely generated abelian group</Term> (it's finite!), so it decomposes as $\mathbb{Z}/q_1\mathbb{Z} \oplus \dots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/q_m\mathbb{Z}$. If $F^\times$ were *not* cyclic, then $L = \operatorname{lcm}(q_1, \dots, q_m)$ would be strictly smaller than $|F^\times|$, yet every element $x$ would satisfy $x^L = 1$ — giving more than $L$ roots of $x^L - 1$, contradicting the counting fact above. So $F^\times$ must be cyclic after all.
