<SlideLayout title="Why the vector space argument works">

Here's the proof sketch, very briefly, since it really is easy once you see it:

- **(a)** Apply Lagrange's theorem (or just pigeonhole) to the additive group $(F,+)$ to see the characteristic can't be $0$.
- **(b)** The additive subgroup generated by $1_F$ — that is, $\{0, 1_F, 1_F+1_F, \dots\}$ — is an isomorphic copy of $\mathbb{F}_p$ sitting inside $F$.
- **(c)** $F$ is then a field extension of $\mathbb{F}_p$, so in particular a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{F}_p$-vector space, with some <Term k="basis">basis</Term> $e_1, \dots, e_n$. Every element of $F$ is a unique $\mathbb{F}_p$-linear combination $c_1 e_1 + \dots + c_n e_n$ — and there are exactly $p^n$ ways to choose the coefficients $c_i \in \mathbb{F}_p$.

That last count is the whole proof: $n$ coordinates, each with $p$ possible values, gives $p^n$ elements total. The <Term k="dimension">dimension</Term> $n$ is called the *degree* of the extension $F/\mathbb{F}_p$.
