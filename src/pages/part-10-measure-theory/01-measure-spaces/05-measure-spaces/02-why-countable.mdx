<SlideLayout title="Why countable additivity?">

The axioms stop at **countable** unions and additivity. Why not uncountable?

<Callout kind="remark" title="Uncountable sums never work">
An uncountable collection of positive real numbers cannot have a finite sum. Here is the elementary reason: if you sum uncountably many positive reals, for each $n$ there must be at least one summand $\geq 1/n$ (else the total would be at most $|\mathbb{N}| \cdot (1/n) = \infty$). Actually, infinitely many summands are $\geq 1/n$ for each $n$, so the total is infinite.
</Callout>

Concretely: the unit interval $[0,1]$ is a union of uncountably many points, each with Lebesgue measure 0. If we required uncountable additivity, we would get $\lambda([0,1]) = 0$ — a contradiction with the obvious $\lambda([0,1]) = 1$.

So <Term k="countableAdditivity">countable additivity</Term> is the right balance:

- **Finite** additivity alone is too weak — we couldn't compute the area of a circle by limits of polygons.
- **Uncountable** additivity would force every set to have measure 0 or $\infty$.
- **Countable** additivity is exactly right for limits and integration.

This is also why we use $\sigma$-algebras (closed under countable unions) and not something larger.

</SlideLayout>
