We will use the adjective **measurable** for the sets that our measure can handle. The family of all measurable sets is denoted by a script letter like <Term k="sigmaAlgebra">$\mathcal{A}$</Term>, and individual measurable sets by early Roman capitals $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, $E$.

This is not a restriction in practice. Here is the good news:

<Callout kind="remark" title="Measurability in practice">
For any set you would write down in a proof or a program — any open set, any closed set, any countable union or intersection of such sets — the set is <Term k="measurableSpace">measurable</Term>. The only non-measurable sets require the Axiom of Choice to construct. You will never encounter one accidentally.
</Callout>

So the theory is not restrictive — it is **protective**. It tells us exactly which sets are safe to work with, and it turns out almost everything is safe.

The formal definition of what makes a family $\mathcal{A}$ "good enough" is the $\sigma$-algebra axioms, which we state next.
