<SlideLayout title="Generated σ-algebras">

In practice we rarely describe a $\sigma$-algebra by listing all its sets. Instead, we **generate** it from a small seed family.

<Callout kind="definition" title="σ-algebra generated by a family">
Let $\Omega$ be a set and $\mathcal{F}$ a family of subsets of $\Omega$. The <Term k="generatedSigmaAlgebra">$\sigma$-algebra generated by $\mathcal{F}$</Term>, written $\sigma(\mathcal{F})$, is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra on $\Omega$ that contains every set in $\mathcal{F}$.
</Callout>

"Smallest" is well-defined because the intersection of any collection of $\sigma$-algebras is again a $\sigma$-algebra. So $\sigma(\mathcal{F})$ is the intersection of all $\sigma$-algebras containing $\mathcal{F}$.

The process is like "closure under the axioms." Start with the open sets of $\mathbb{R}$: throw in all complements (closed sets), then all countable unions, then all complements of those, and so on — forever. The exact shape is impossible to describe explicitly, so we just say "start with open sets, shake vigorously."

<Callout kind="remark" title="You don't need to see the result">
When working with generated $\sigma$-algebras, you rarely need to know which sets are in them. You just need to know the generating family and use the axioms. This "just let the algebra close up" idiom is common across mathematics.
</Callout>
