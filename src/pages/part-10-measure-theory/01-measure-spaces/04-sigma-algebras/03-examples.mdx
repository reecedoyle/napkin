<SlideLayout title="Examples of σ-algebras">

Three concrete $\sigma$-algebras, from coarsest to most useful.

<Callout kind="example" title="Three σ-algebras">

**(a) Power set.** For any $\Omega$, the <Term k="powerSet">power set $2^\Omega$</Term> (all subsets) is trivially a $\sigma$-algebra. Fine for finite or countably infinite $\Omega$; too large to put a sensible measure on when $\Omega = \mathbb{R}$.

**(b) Countable/co-countable algebra.** On an uncountable $\Omega$, declare $A \in \mathcal{A}$ if $A$ is countable or $\Omega \setminus A$ is countable. This is a very coarse $\sigma$-algebra — most sets don't appear.

**(c) Borel $\sigma$-algebra.** If $\Omega$ is a topological space, the <Term k="borelSigmaAlgebra">Borel $\sigma$-algebra</Term> $\mathcal{B}(\Omega)$ is generated by all open sets. The pair $(\Omega, \mathcal{B}(\Omega))$ is called a <Term k="borelSpace">Borel space</Term>. For $\Omega = \mathbb{R}$ this is the σ-algebra we'll use almost always.
</Callout>

**A quick check:** Closed sets are in $\mathcal{B}(\Omega)$ because they are complements of open sets, and $\sigma$-algebras are closed under complements. The half-open interval $[0, 1)$ is in $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ since $[0,1) = \bigcap_n (- 1/n, 1)$, a countable intersection of open sets.
