<SlideLayout title="Lebesgue generalizes Riemann">

The good news: for most functions you already know how to integrate, the two integrals agree.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Lebesgue integral generalizes Riemann integral">
Let $f \colon [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be <Term k="riemannIntegrable">Riemann integrable</Term>, where $[a,b]$ is equipped with the <Term k="borelMeasure">Borel measure</Term>. Then $f$ is also Lebesgue integrable, and

$$\int_a^b f(x) \; dx = \int_{[a,b]} f \; d\mu.$$
</Callout>

In practice: **we do all the theory with Lebesgue integrals, but we compute them with Riemann tools.**

When you need to evaluate $\int_{[1,4]} x^2 \; d\mu$, you just revert to the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$\int_{[1,4]} x^2 \; d\mu = \int_1^4 x^2 \; dx = \frac{4^3}{3} - \frac{1^3}{3} = 21.$$

No new computation techniques are needed. The Lebesgue theory is richer (more functions are integrable, better limit theorems), but the actual arithmetic is unchanged.
