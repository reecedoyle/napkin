<SlideLayout title="Lebesgue integration — overview">

We have a <Term k="measureSpace">measure space $(\Omega, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$</Term> and a function $f \colon \Omega \to [0,\infty]$. We want to assign a number

$$\int_\Omega f \; d\mu$$

called the <Term k="lebesgueIntegral">Lebesgue integral</Term> of $f$ with respect to $\mu$.

This integral may be $+\infty$. The construction works in four steps, each extending the previous one:

1. **Indicator functions** — we know exactly how much a set "weighs".
2. **Simple functions** — finite linear combinations of indicators.
3. **Nonnegative functions** — approximate from below by simple functions.
4. **Absolutely integrable functions** — split into positive and negative parts.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Why bother?">
The Riemann integral works by slicing the *domain* (the x-axis) into thin strips. The Lebesgue integral instead slices the *range* (the y-axis). This change of perspective makes the theory far more powerful — and lets us integrate many functions that Riemann cannot handle.
</Callout>
