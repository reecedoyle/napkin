<SlideLayout title="Algebras of sets">

A <Term k="sigmaAlgebra">$\sigma$-algebra</Term> is closed under countable unions. An algebra is the same idea but only requires closure under **finite** unions.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Algebra">
An **algebra** $\mathcal{A}_0$ on a set $\Omega$ is a nonempty subset of $2^\Omega$ that is closed under complement and **finite** union.

The smallest algebra containing a family $\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^\Omega$ is called the **algebra generated by $\mathcal{F}$**.
</Callout>

Every $\sigma$-algebra is an algebra, but not vice versa. The difference: a $\sigma$-algebra can handle countably infinite operations; an algebra handles only finitely many at a time.

<Callout kind="example" title="The rectangle algebra on ℝⁿ">
Take $\Omega = \mathbb{R}^n$. Let $\mathcal{L}_0$ be the algebra generated by rectangular prisms $[a_1,b_1]\times\cdots\times[a_n,b_n]$.

A typical element of $\mathcal{L}_0$ is a **finite** union of rectangles and their complements — a shape you can definitely assign an area to, because only finitely many pieces are involved.
</Callout>
