<SlideLayout title="Fourier inversion in the non-compact case">

Even without an $L^2$ basis, we can still define Fourier coefficients and recover $f$ from them.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Fourier inversion formula (non-compact case)">
Let $G$ be an LCA group with Haar measure $\mu$. For $f \colon G \to \CC$ and $\xi \in \wh G$, define
$$\wh f(\xi) = \int_G f(x)\, \ol{e_\xi(x)}\, d\mu.$$
Then there exists a unique Haar measure $\nu$ on $\wh G$ (the <Term k="dualMeasure">**dual measure**</Term>) such that: whenever $f \in L^1(G)$ and $\wh f \in L^1(\wh G)$, we have
$$f(x) = \int_{\wh G} \wh f(\xi)\, \xi(x)\, d\nu(\xi)$$
for $\mu$-almost all $x \in G$. If $f$ is continuous, this holds everywhere.
</Callout>

The formula $f(x) = \int_{\wh G} \wh f(\xi)\, \xi(x)\, d\nu$ is a perfect analogue of the compact case's series $f = \sum_\xi \wh f(\xi)\, e_\xi$ — the sum has become an integral, and counting measure has become the dual measure $\nu$.

<Callout kind="remark" title="The dual measure for compact G">
When $G$ is compact, the dual measure on the discrete $\wh G$ is the **counting measure** — consistent with the Parseval identity $\|f\|^2 = \sum_\xi |\wh f(\xi)|^2$ from the compact theorem.
</Callout>

</SlideLayout>
