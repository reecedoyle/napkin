<SlideLayout title="Topological groups">

When we studied Fourier series, the domain was the circle <Term k="circleGroup">$\TT$</Term>.
When we studied the Fourier transform, the domain was <Term k="RR">$\RR$</Term>.
Both spaces share something beyond being topological spaces: they are also **abelian groups**.

A structure that is simultaneously a group and a topological space — where the two interact nicely — is called a topological group.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Topological group">
A <Term k="topologicalGroup">**topological group**</Term> is a Hausdorff topological space $G$ equipped with a group operation such that both maps

$$G \times G \to G,\quad (x,y)\mapsto xy \qquad\text{and}\qquad G \to G,\quad x\mapsto x\inv$$

are continuous.
</Callout>

The Hausdorff condition says distinct points can be separated by open sets — a mild sanity check most spaces of interest satisfy. The continuity conditions say you can't "teleport" when you multiply or invert.

**Examples:** $(\RR, +)$, $(\CC \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)$, and the circle group $\TT = \{z \in \CC : |z|=1\}$ under multiplication are all topological groups. Any group with the discrete topology (every subset open) is also a topological group.

</SlideLayout>
