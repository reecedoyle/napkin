<Callout kind="example" title="Computing a limit of integrals via DCT">
We want $\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_{(0,1)} \frac{n\sin(n^{-1} x)}{\sqrt{x}}\; dx$.

**Step 1 — find the pointwise limit.**
For fixed $x > 0$: $n\sin(n^{-1}x) = n \cdot \frac{x}{n}\bigl(1 + O(n^{-2})\bigr) \to x$.
So $\dfrac{n\sin(n^{-1}x)}{\sqrt{x}} \to \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x}} = \sqrt{x}$.

**Step 2 — find a dominator.**
We have $\bigl\lvert\sin(\theta)\bigr\rvert \le \lvert\theta\rvert$,
so $n\lvert\sin(n^{-1}x)\rvert \le x$.
Thus $\bigl\lvert f_n(x)\bigr\rvert = \dfrac{n|\sin(n^{-1}x)|}{\sqrt{x}} \le \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x}} = \sqrt{x}$.
The function $g(x) = \sqrt{x}$ is absolutely integrable on $(0,1)$.

**Step 3 — apply DCT.** By the <Term k="dominatedConvergence">dominated convergence theorem</Term>:
$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_{(0,1)} \frac{n\sin(n^{-1}x)}{\sqrt{x}}\; dx = \int_{(0,1)} \sqrt{x}\; dx = \frac{2}{3}.$$
</Callout>
