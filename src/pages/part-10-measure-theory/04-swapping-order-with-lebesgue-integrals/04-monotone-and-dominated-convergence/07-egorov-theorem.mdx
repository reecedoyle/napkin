<SlideLayout title="Egorov's theorem">

The counterexamples all had their "bad behavior" concentrated in a specific region.
Egorov's theorem makes this precise.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Egorov's theorem">
Let $(\Omega, \mu)$ be a measure space with $\mu(\Omega) < \infty$.
Let $f_1, f_2, \dots$ and $f \colon \Omega \to \RR$ be measurable with $f_n \to f$ pointwise
almost everywhere. For any $\eps > 0$ there exists a measurable $U \subseteq \Omega$ such that:
$$\mu(\Omega \setminus U) < \eps,$$
and the convergence of $f_n \to f$ is <Term k="uniformConvergence">uniform</Term> on $U$.
</Callout>

In words: the failure of uniform convergence is always confined to a set of measure less than $\eps$.
Delete that small set and everything is well-behaved.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Uniform convergence theorem">
If $f_n \to f$ uniformly on a finite-measure space, then $f$ is integrable and
$\lim_n \int f_n\; d\mu = \int f\; d\mu$.
</Callout>

Together these say: the reason $\int f\; d\mu \neq \lim \int f_k\; d\mu$ is always explained by
the bad behavior on the exceptional set $\Omega \setminus U$. Remove it, and the interchange is valid.

</SlideLayout>
