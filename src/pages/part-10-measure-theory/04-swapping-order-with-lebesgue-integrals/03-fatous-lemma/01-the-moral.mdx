<SlideLayout title="Fatou's lemma — the moral">

Look back at all the counterexamples: the Witch's hat, the spreading uniform mass.
In every case, $\int f_k \; dx$ was *larger* than $\int f \; dx$ — the integral of the limit.

We can make the anomaly go the other way by negating the functions,
but for *nonneg.* functions the discrepancy can only run in one direction.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Moral of Fatou's lemma">
For nonneg. functions, if the interchange fails then the integral of the limit is *smaller*
than the limit of the integrals — mass can escape to infinity or concentrate on shrinking spikes,
but it cannot appear from nowhere.

In symbols: $\displaystyle\int f\; d\mu \le \lim_{n\to\infty}\int f_n\; d\mu$.
</Callout>

This is the essential content of <Term k="fatousLemma">Fatou's lemma</Term>.
It turns out the correct way to state it uses the <Term k="liminf">$\liminf$</Term>,
which generalises the plain limit to sequences that might not converge.

</SlideLayout>
