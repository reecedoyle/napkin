<SlideLayout title="The abelianization of an algebra">

The observation $\chi_V(ab) = \chi_V(ba)$ means every character vanishes on all commutators $ab - ba$.
This motivates quotienting out by the <Term k="commutatorSubspace">commutator subspace</Term> $[A, A]$.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Abelianization">
The <Term k="abelianizationAlg">**abelianization**</Term> of an algebra $A$ is
$$A^{\mathrm{ab}} \coloneqq A / [A, A]$$
where $[A, A]$ is the $k$-subspace spanned by $\{xy - yx : x, y \in A\}$.
Each character $\chi_V$ factors through $A^{\mathrm{ab}}$, so we may view it as a linear functional in $(A^{\mathrm{ab}})^\vee$.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" title="Three abelianizations">
**(a)** If $A$ is commutative, $[A,A] = \{0\}$ and $A^{\mathrm{ab}} = A$.

**(b)** $A = \Mat_k(d)$: the commutator subspace is exactly the trace-zero matrices,
so $A^{\mathrm{ab}}$ is one-dimensional (spanned by the identity).

**(c)** $A = k[G]$: in $A^{\mathrm{ab}}$ we identify $gh \sim hg$ for all $g, h \in G$, i.e.\ $ghg^{-1} \sim h$.
So $A^{\mathrm{ab}}$ is the space of $k$-linear combinations of **conjugacy classes** of $G$.
</Callout>

</SlideLayout>
