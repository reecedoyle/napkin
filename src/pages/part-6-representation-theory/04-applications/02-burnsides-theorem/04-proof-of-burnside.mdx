<SlideLayout title="Proof of Burnside's theorem">

With both lemmas in hand, Burnside's theorem is a short counting argument.

**Proof.** Suppose for contradiction that $G$ is simple, nonabelian, $|G| = p^a q^b$.

Partition $G$ into its <Term k="conjugacyClass">conjugacy classes</Term>. Each class has size either:
- **1** — these are exactly the elements of the centre $Z(G)$; there are $|Z(G)|$ of them.
- **divisible by $pq$** — because by the lemma no class can have size $p^k$ or $q^k$ with $k > 0$.

Since $|G| = \sum |C_i|$, we get $|Z(G)| \equiv |G| = p^a q^b \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$.

But $p \mid |Z(G)|$ and $|Z(G)| \ge 1$ forces $|Z(G)| > 1$. So the centre is nontrivial. Since $G$ is nonabelian, $Z(G) \neq G$. Thus $Z(G)$ is a proper nontrivial normal subgroup of $G$, contradicting simplicity. $\square$
