<SlideLayout title="Density theorem — proof for one irrep">

The proof for a single irrep $V$ is the heart of the argument.

Fix a basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$ of $V$. Suppose for contradiction the map $\rho \colon A \to \Mat(V)$ is not surjective. The map $\Reg(A) \to V^{\oplus n}$ given by $a \mapsto (a \cdot e_1, \dots, a \cdot e_n)$ would then be a representation morphism whose image is a **proper** subrepresentation of $V^{\oplus n}$.

By the <Term k="completelyReducible">Schur corollary</Term>, this image is isomorphic to $V^{\oplus m}$ for some $m < n$, with the inclusion given by a matrix of constants $X$.

<Callout kind="remark" title="The linear algebra contradiction">
Since $m < n$, the matrix $X$ (of size $n \times m$) has a nonzero vector $(c_1, \dots, c_n)$ in its left kernel. Then

$$\sum_{i=1}^n c_i e_i = [c_1 \; \cdots \; c_n] \cdot X \cdot [v_1 \; \cdots \; v_m]^\top = 0,$$

contradicting the linear independence of the basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$.
</Callout>

Therefore the map is surjective for $r = 1$. For $r \ge 2$, the same argument applies block by block using the Schur corollary for each irrep separately.

</SlideLayout>
