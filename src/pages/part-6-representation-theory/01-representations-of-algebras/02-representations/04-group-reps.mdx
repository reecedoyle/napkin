<SlideLayout title="Group representations via the group algebra">

The most important example. Let $A = k[S_3]$ and $V = \mathbb{R}^{\oplus 3} = \{(x,y,z)\}$. Each permutation $\pi \in S_3$ acts by permuting coordinates:

$$(1\;2) \cdot (x,y,z) = (y,x,z).$$

In matrix form, $(1\;2)$ maps to the permutation matrix $\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$.

More generally:

<Callout kind="definition" title="Group representation">
A <Term k="algRepresentation">representation</Term> $(V, \rho)$ of the group algebra $k[G]$ is exactly a <Term k="groupAlgebra">group homomorphism</Term> $\psi \colon G \to \GL(V)$. We call this a **group representation** of $G$. In particular, $\rho(1_G) = \mathrm{id}_V$.
</Callout>

So studying representations of the algebra $k[G]$ is the same as studying group representations of $G$.

<Callout kind="example" title="Regular representation">
Any $k$-algebra $A$ has a <Term k="regularRep">**regular representation**</Term> $\mathrm{Reg}(A)$: the algebra $A$ acts on itself by left multiplication, $a \cdot b = ab$.
</Callout>
