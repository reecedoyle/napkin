<SlideLayout title="Infinite decimals as limits">

Monotone convergence makes "infinite decimals" rigorous. The sequence of finite truncations $0.9$, $0.99$, $0.999$, $\ldots$ is non-decreasing and bounded above by $1$. So it converges — and its limit is obviously $1$.

<Callout kind="example" title="0.999… = 1">
We *define* the repeating decimal $0.9999\ldots$ to be the limit of the sequence $0.9,\ 0.99,\ 0.999,\ \ldots$. Each term is $1 - 10^{-n}$, and

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 - 10^{-n}\right) = 1.$$

So $0.9999\ldots = 1$. This is not a trick — it is a definition.
</Callout>

More generally, any infinite decimal is a limit of its finite truncations. The truncations form a non-decreasing bounded sequence, so monotone convergence guarantees the limit exists. Real numbers *are* infinite decimals, and now we know why that makes sense.
