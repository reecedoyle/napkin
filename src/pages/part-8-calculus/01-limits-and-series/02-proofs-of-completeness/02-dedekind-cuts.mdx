<SlideLayout title="Dedekind cuts — where the real numbers come from">

We've shown sup's imply completeness. But why does $\RR$ *have* sup's? Because we've never actually given a rigorous construction of $\RR$.

The slickest approach uses <Term k="dedekindCut">Dedekind cuts</Term>.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Dedekind cut">
A **Dedekind cut** is a pair $A \mid B$ of subsets of $\QQ$ satisfying:
- $\QQ = A \sqcup B$ (they partition $\QQ$);
- both $A$ and $B$ are nonempty;
- $a < b$ for every $a \in A$ and $b \in B$; and
- $A$ has no largest element (i.e. $\sup A \notin A$).

We *define* a real number to be a Dedekind cut.
</Callout>

Picture a knife slicing the rational number line: everything to the left is $A$, everything to the right is $B$. If the knife lands at a rational, we put that rational in $B$ by the last condition.

With this definition, sup's are easy: to take $\sup$ of a set of real numbers, take the union of all their left halves $A$. The tricky part is defining $+$, $\times$, etc. on these awkward pairs — but it works.
