<SlideLayout title="Infinite series are limits of partial sums">

How do we "add infinitely many numbers"? We don't. Instead, we take a limit.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Convergent series">
Given a sequence $a_1, a_2, \ldots$, the **partial sums** are

$$s_n = a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n.$$

The <Term k="series">series</Term> $\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k$ **converges** to $L$ if $\lim_{n\to\infty} s_n = L$. Otherwise, it **diverges**.
</Callout>

There is no actual infinite addition. The infinite sum is *defined* to be the limit of the finite partial sums. This lets us write, for example,

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$$

without any mystery — the partial sums $\frac{1}{1} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{9} + \cdots$ converge to a real number, and that number happens to be $\frac{\pi^2}{6}$. (We won't prove that here; just note that it's a statement about a sequence of partial sums.)

</SlideLayout>
