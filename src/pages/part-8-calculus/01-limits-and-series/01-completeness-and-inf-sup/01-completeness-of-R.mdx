<SlideLayout title="Completeness of ℝ">

Real analysis is built on two foundational facts about <Term k="RR">$\RR$</Term>. We've actually seen the first one already, back when we studied metric spaces.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="ℝ is complete">
As a metric space, <Term k="RR">$\RR$</Term> is **complete**: every <Term k="cauchySequence">Cauchy sequence</Term> in $\RR$ converges to a limit in $\RR$.
</Callout>

Recall what "Cauchy" means: the terms get arbitrarily close to *each other*, without needing to know the limit in advance. Completeness says: in $\RR$, that's enough to guarantee convergence.

The classic failure case is <Term k="QQ">$\QQ$</Term>: the sequence $1,\ 1.4,\ 1.41,\ 1.414,\ \ldots$ (successive decimal truncations of $\sqrt{2}$) is Cauchy in $\QQ$ but its limit $\sqrt{2}$ isn't rational. So $\QQ$ is not complete.
