<SlideLayout title="log and general exponentiation">

Since $\exp \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is continuous, strictly increasing, and surjective onto the positive reals, it has an inverse.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Natural logarithm">
Define $\log \colon \mathbb{R}_{>0} \to \mathbb{R}$ to be the inverse function of $\exp$. Its derivative is $1/x$, and a direct computation of its Taylor coefficients shows it is <Term k="analyticFunction">analytic</Term>.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="definition" title="General real exponentiation">
For any $a > 0$ and $r \in \mathbb{R}$, we define

$$a^r \coloneqq \exp(r \log a).$$
</Callout>

This definition is *rigorous*: it relies only on $\exp$ and $\log$, which we have built from power series. It recovers all the familiar laws ($a^{r+s} = a^r a^s$, etc.) from the functional equation of $\exp$.
