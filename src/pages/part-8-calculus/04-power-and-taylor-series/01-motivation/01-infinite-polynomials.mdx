<SlideLayout title="Infinite polynomials">

Polynomials are the friendliest functions we know. They are smooth, easy to differentiate, and their behaviour is completely determined by finitely many coefficients.

What if we allowed *infinitely many* coefficients?

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n x^n = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3 + \cdots$$

Such a thing is called a <Term k="powerSeries">power series</Term>. This chapter is about three remarkable facts that make them almost as well-behaved as ordinary polynomials:

1. We can read off exactly which inputs $x$ make the series converge — there is always a <Term k="radiusOfConvergence">radius of convergence</Term> $R$.
2. We can differentiate term by term, just like polynomials.
3. Many functions you already know — $e^x$, $\sin x$, $\log x$ — **are** power series in disguise.
