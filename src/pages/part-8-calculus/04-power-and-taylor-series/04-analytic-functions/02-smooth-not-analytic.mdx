<SlideLayout title="A smooth function that is not analytic">

Being <Term k="smoothFunction">smooth</Term> (infinitely differentiable) is weaker than being <Term k="analyticFunction">analytic</Term>. The classic example:

<Callout kind="example" title="Smooth but not analytic">
Define

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/x} & x > 0 \\ 0 & x \le 0. \end{cases}$$

One can show that $f^{(n)}(0) = 0$ for every $n$. So its <Term k="taylorSeries">Taylor series</Term> at $0$ is identically $0 + 0 \cdot x + 0 \cdot x^2 + \cdots$.

That series *does* converge everywhere — but it converges to $0$, not to $f$. Since $f(\varepsilon) = e^{-1/\varepsilon} > 0$ for any $\varepsilon > 0$, the Taylor series fails to represent $f$ near $0$.
</Callout>

The lesson: knowing a function is smooth and knowing all its derivatives at one point is *not enough* to recover the function's values elsewhere. Analytic functions are the special ones where it *is* enough.

</SlideLayout>
