<SlideLayout title="Non-examples of uniform continuity">

To build intuition, let's look at two classic failures — and one surprising success.

<Callout kind="example" title="Uniform continuity failures">

**(a) $f(x) = x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$.**
Take $\eps = 0.1$. However small we choose $\delta$, large values of $x$ and $y = x + \delta/2$ satisfy $|x - y| < \delta$ but
$$|x^2 - y^2| = |x+y| \cdot |x-y| \approx 2|x| \cdot \frac\delta2 = |x|\delta,$$
which grows without bound as $x \to \infty$. No single $\delta$ works everywhere.

**(b) $f(x) = 1/x$ on $(0,1)$.**
Near $x = 0$ the function explodes — the derivative is $-1/x^2$, unbounded — so again, no global $\delta$ can tame it.

**(c) $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ on $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ — but this one IS uniformly continuous!**
Even though the derivative $1/(2\sqrt{x})$ blows up near 0, the function itself stays bounded in oscillation. One can check:
$$|x - y| < \eps^2 \implies |\sqrt{x} - \sqrt{y}| < \eps.$$
Unbounded derivative does **not** imply failure of uniform continuity.

</Callout>

The lesson: uniform continuity is about how much $f$ *can fluctuate* across the domain, not about slopes at individual points.

</SlideLayout>
