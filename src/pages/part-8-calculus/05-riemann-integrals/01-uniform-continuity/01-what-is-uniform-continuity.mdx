<SlideLayout title="What is uniform continuity?">

We already know what it means for a function to be continuous: at every point $p$, we can make $f(p)$ and $f(q)$ close by making $p$ and $q$ close. The catch is that "how close" depends on *where we are* — near $p$.

<Term k="uniformlyContinuous">**Uniform continuity**</Term> demands more: a single $\delta$ must work for *all* points simultaneously.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Uniformly continuous">
A map $f \colon M \to N$ between <Term k="metricSpace">metric spaces</Term> is **uniformly continuous** if for every $\eps > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that
$$d_M(p, q) < \delta \implies d_N(f(p), f(q)) < \eps$$
for **every** pair of inputs $p, q \in M$.
</Callout>

In ordinary continuity, $\delta$ is allowed to depend on the point $p$ (so steep parts of the curve demand a smaller $\delta$). Uniform continuity insists the same $\delta$ serves the whole domain at once.
