The <Term k="riemannIntegral">Riemann integral</Term> connects to differentiation via the fundamental theorem of calculus. Here is the version we can now prove.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Fundamental theorem of calculus">
Let $f \colon [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous, differentiable on $(a,b)$, with $f'$ extending to a continuous function on $[a,b]$. Then
$$\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx = f(b) - f(a).$$
</Callout>

**The idea via tagged partitions:** for any $n$, partition $[a,b]$ into $n$ equal pieces of width $w = (b-a)/n$. By the Mean Value Theorem, on each piece $[t_{i-1}, t_i]$ there exists $\xi_i$ with
$$f'(\xi_i) \cdot w = f(t_i) - f(t_{i-1}).$$
Summing over all $i$, the Riemann sum telescopes:
$$\sum_{i=1}^n f'(\xi_i)\,w = f(b) - f(a).$$
As $n \to \infty$ (mesh $\to 0$), the left side converges to $\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx$, giving the result.

<Callout kind="example" title="AP calculus returns">
$\int_1^4 x^2\,dx$: let $F(x) = x^3/3$, so $F'(x) = x^2$. By FTC:
$$\int_1^4 x^2\,dx = F(4) - F(1) = \tfrac{64}{3} - \tfrac{1}{3} = 21.$$
</Callout>
