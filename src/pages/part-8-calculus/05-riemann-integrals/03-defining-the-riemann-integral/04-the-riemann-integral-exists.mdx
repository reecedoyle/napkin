<SlideLayout title="The Riemann integral — existence and uniqueness">

We now have all three ingredients: $R([a,b])$ is dense in $M([a,b])$, $\Sigma$ is uniformly continuous on $R([a,b])$, and $\mathbb{R}$ is complete. The extension theorem delivers the integral.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="The Riemann integral">
There exists a unique continuous map
$$\textstyle\int_a^b \colon M([a,b]) \to \mathbb{R}$$
such that $\int_a^b f = \Sigma(f)$ for every rectangle function $f \in R([a,b])$.
</Callout>

The diagram says it plainly: on the big space $M([a,b])$, the integral $\int_a^b$ is *the* unique continuous extension of the rectangle-summing map $\Sigma$. We didn't pick a convention, we didn't choose which Riemann sums to use — the extension theorem forces the answer.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Why this approach?">
Most calculus courses define the integral as a limit of Riemann sums. That works too, and in the next section we'll see these approaches agree. But the extension-theorem route is conceptually cleaner: it reduces the integral to two facts — (1) rectangle functions are dense, (2) area-summing is uniformly continuous — plus the machinery we already built.
</Callout>
