<SlideLayout title="Dense subsets">

A subset $S$ of a topological space $X$ is <Term k="denseSubset">**dense**</Term> if it "touches" every open region of $X$ — no open set avoids it entirely.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Dense subset">
A subset $S \subseteq X$ of a topological space $X$ is **dense** if every nonempty open subset of $X$ contains at least one point of $S$.
</Callout>

Equivalently (for metric spaces): $S$ is dense in $X$ iff every point of $X$ is a limit of a sequence in $S$.

<Callout kind="example" title="Dense sets in practice">

- $\mathbb{Q}$ is <Term k="denseSubset">dense</Term> in $\mathbb{R}$: between any two reals lies a rational.
- Any metric space $M$ is dense in its completion $\overline{M}$. This is essentially the definition of completion.

</Callout>

Dense subsets matter because they determine functions completely: if two continuous functions agree on a dense set, they agree everywhere.
