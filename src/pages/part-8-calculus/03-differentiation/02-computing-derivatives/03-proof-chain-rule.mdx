The chain rule proof has one subtle point: the denominator $f(p+h) - f(p)$ might be zero even for small nonzero $h$, so we cannot naively divide by it.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Proof">
Let $p \in U$, $q = f(p)$. Define the auxiliary function
$$Q(y) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{g(y) - g(q)}{y - q} & y \neq q \\ g'(q) & y = q. \end{cases}$$
Since $g$ is differentiable at $q$, $Q$ is continuous at $q$.

Now for any $h$, whether or not $f(p+h) = q$:
$$\frac{g(f(p+h)) - g(q)}{h} = Q(f(p+h)) \cdot \frac{f(p+h) - f(p)}{h}.$$
(If $f(p+h) = q$ the left side is $0$, and the right side is $Q(q) \cdot 0 = 0$.)

As $h \to 0$: the second factor $\to f'(p)$, and $Q(f(p+h)) \to Q(q) = g'(q)$ by continuity of $Q$ and continuity of $f$ at $p$. So $(g \circ f)'(p) = g'(q) \cdot f'(p) = g'(f(p)) \cdot f'(p)$.
</Callout>
