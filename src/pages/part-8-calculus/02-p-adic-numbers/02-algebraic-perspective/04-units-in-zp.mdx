<SlideLayout title="Units in ℤₚ — non-multiples of p">

In the power series ring $K[\![X]\!]$, a series $F$ is invertible if and only if $F(0) \neq 0$. There is a perfect $p$-adic analogue:

<Callout kind="proposition" title="Units of ℤₚ">
An element $x \in \mathbb{Z}_p$ is a unit (has a multiplicative inverse in $\mathbb{Z}_p$) if and only if $x \not\equiv 0 \pmod{p}$.
In symbols: $x \in \mathbb{Z}_p^\times \iff x_1 \neq 0$.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" title="−½ ∈ ℤ₃">
We claimed $(1 \bmod 3,\; 4 \bmod 9,\; 13 \bmod 27,\; 40 \bmod 81,\; \ldots) = \overline{\ldots 1111}_3$.
Multiplying by $-2$ gives $(1 \bmod 3,\; 1 \bmod 9,\; 1 \bmod 27,\; \ldots) = 1$.
So $\overline{\ldots 1111}_3 = -\tfrac{1}{2} \in \mathbb{Z}_3$!
</Callout>

<Callout kind="remark" title="½ is an integer when p > 2">
Since $2 \not\equiv 0 \pmod{3}$, the proposition says $2$ is a unit in <Term k="Zp">$\mathbb{Z}_3$</Term>, so $\tfrac{1}{2} \in \mathbb{Z}_3$. Your intuition about "what is an integer" changes here.
</Callout>
