<Callout kind="theorem" title="Algebraic integers form a ring">
The set $\ol{\ZZ}$ of all algebraic integers is closed under addition and multiplication — it is a ring. In particular, $\OO_K$ is a ring for any number field $K$.
</Callout>

<ProofReveal
  client:load
  id="an54-s2-proof-ok-ring"
  prompt="Why is 𝒪_K closed under + and ×? (Hint: think about finitely generated modules.)"
  solution="Let α, β ∈ Z̄ (algebraic integers). By the lemma, ℤ[α] and ℤ[β] are finitely generated as ℤ-modules, with ℤ-bases {a₁,…,aₘ} and {b₁,…,bₙ} respectively. Consider the ring ℤ[α, β]: it is generated by all products aᵢbⱼ, so it is finitely generated by mn elements. Since ℤ[α ± β] and ℤ[αβ] are subsets of ℤ[α, β], they too are finitely generated (submodules of a Noetherian ring). By the lemma, α ± β and αβ are algebraic integers."
/>

<Callout kind="example" title="½ is not an algebraic integer">
We already know $\frac{1}{2} \notin \ol{\ZZ}$. Consistent with the lemma:
$$\ZZ\!\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right] = \left\{\frac{a}{2^m} \;\middle|\; a, m \in \ZZ,\ m \geq 0\right\}$$
is **not** finitely generated as a $\ZZ$-module — you need arbitrarily high powers of $\tfrac{1}{2}$.
</Callout>
