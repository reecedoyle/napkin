Why is the norm multiplicative? The cleanest explanation comes from viewing $K$ as a <Term k="vectorSpace">$\QQ$-vector space</Term> and the norm as a determinant.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Morally correct definition of norm and trace">
Let $K$ be a number field of degree $n$ and $\alpha \in K$. Define the $\QQ$-linear map
$$\mu_\alpha \colon K \to K, \quad x \mapsto \alpha x.$$
Then:
$$\operatorname{N}_{K/\QQ}(\alpha) = \det(\mu_\alpha), \qquad \operatorname{Tr}_{K/\QQ}(\alpha) = \operatorname{Tr}(\mu_\alpha).$$
</Callout>

The determinant measures how multiplication by $\alpha$ stretches volume in $K \cong \QQ^n$. Since $\det$ and $\operatorname{Tr}$ are independent of the choice of <Term k="basis">basis</Term>, you can pick any convenient basis and use the usual formulas.

Multiplicativity of the norm is now immediate:
$$\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \mu_\alpha \circ \mu_\beta \implies \det(\mu_{\alpha\beta}) = \det(\mu_\alpha)\cdot\det(\mu_\beta),$$
because determinants multiply under composition. Similarly, the trace is additive.
