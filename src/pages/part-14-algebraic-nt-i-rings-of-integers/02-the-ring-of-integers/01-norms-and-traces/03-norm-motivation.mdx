---
title: Why the norm depends on the field
---
import SlideLayout from '@/layouts/SlideLayout.astro';
import Callout from '@/components/Callout.astro';

<SlideLayout title="Why the norm depends on the field">

It's tempting to define the **norm** of $\alpha$ as the product of all its conjugates. For $2 + i \in \QQ(i)$ that gives $(2+i)(2-i) = 5$, which is the familiar $a^2 + b^2$.

But there's a subtlety. We want the norm to be **multiplicative**: $N(\alpha\beta) = N(\alpha)\cdot N(\beta)$. Let's check:

$$N(2+i) = 5, \quad N(2-i) = 5.$$

But $(2+i)(2-i) = 5$, which is a degree-$1$ rational number with **no conjugates at all** other than itself. If we only take the product of conjugates, $N(5) = 5$, not $25$.

<Callout kind="remark" title="The fix: norm is relative to a field">
The norm is not a property of $\alpha$ alone — it is a property of $\alpha$ viewed **inside a particular number field** $K$.

If $5$ lives in $\QQ$, its norm is $5$. But viewed as an element of $\QQ(i)$, its norm should be $25$ — because $\QQ(i)$ "sees" $5$ as having a repeated conjugate.
</Callout>

This motivates defining $\operatorname{N}_{K/\QQ}(\alpha)$, the norm of $\alpha$ **with respect to $K$**.

</SlideLayout>
