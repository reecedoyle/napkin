<SlideLayout title="Proof sketch — why ideals fix what elements broke">

In $\ZZ$, unique factorization of integers follows this template:
1. Show $p \mid bc \Rightarrow p \mid b$ or $p \mid c$ (Euclid's Lemma, from the definition of prime).
2. Show every $N > 1$ factors into primes (induction on size).
3. Uniqueness: if $p_1 \cdots p_m = q_1 \cdots q_n$, then $p_1 = q_i$ for some $i$ by step 1.
4. Divide both sides by $p_1$ and induct.

For ideals in a Dedekind domain, steps 1 and 3 are free (we use the ideal-world definition of prime). But steps 2 and 4 need new ideas:

- **Step 2** uses Zorn's Lemma (because we have no notion of size for abstract ideals).
- **Step 4** requires inverting ideals — we need $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}$ such that $\mathfrak{p} \cdot \mathfrak{p}^{-1} = (1)$.

<Callout kind="remark" title="The inverse trick">
The inverse $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}$ is not an ordinary ideal of $\mathcal{A}$ — it lives in the fraction field $K$.
We call it a <Term k="an55fractionalIdeal">fractional ideal</Term>. Proving $\mathfrak{p} \cdot \mathfrak{p}^{-1} = (1)$ is the nontrivial step, and it relies on $\mathcal{A}$ being a Dedekind domain.
</Callout>

The high-level plan:
1. Show every ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ divides $\mathfrak{p}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{p}_g$ for some finite collection of prime ideals.
2. Define $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}$ and prove $\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{p}^{-1} = (1)$.
3. Prove existence of factorization.
4. Prove uniqueness using inverses.
