<SlideLayout title="What is the discriminant?">

Every number field $K$ of degree $n$ carries a single integer called its discriminant. Think of it as a measure of how "spread out" or "ramified" the field is — small absolute value means the field sits close to $\QQ$, large absolute value signals high arithmetic complexity.

We define it via the embeddings. For a degree-$n$ field $K$, there are exactly $n$ field embeddings $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_n : K \hookrightarrow \CC$. Pick any <Term k="ZZ">$\ZZ$</Term>-basis $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$ for $\OO_K$ and form the **embedding matrix**:

$$
M \;=\;
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_1(\alpha_1) & \cdots & \sigma_n(\alpha_1) \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\sigma_1(\alpha_n) & \cdots & \sigma_n(\alpha_n)
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

<Callout kind="definition" title="Discriminant Δ_K">
The discriminant of $K$ is
$$
\Delta_K \;\defeq\; \det(M)^2.
$$
It is an integer, independent of the choice of $\ZZ$-basis.
</Callout>

Changing the basis replaces $M$ by $M \cdot A$ for an integer matrix $A$ with $\det A = \pm 1$ (a change-of-basis matrix in <Term k="ZZ">$\ZZ$</Term>), so $\det(MA)^2 = \det(M)^2 \det(A)^2 = \det(M)^2$. Basis-independence is immediate.

</SlideLayout>
