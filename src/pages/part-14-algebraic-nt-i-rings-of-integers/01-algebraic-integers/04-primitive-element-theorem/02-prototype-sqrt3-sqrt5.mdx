The prototypical example makes the theorem feel natural.

**Claim:** $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5}) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3} + \sqrt{5})$.

The inclusion $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3} + \sqrt{5}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5})$ is clear — $\sqrt{3} + \sqrt{5}$ is an element of the larger field.

The hard direction: show $\sqrt{3}$ and $\sqrt{5}$ each lie in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3}+\sqrt{5})$.

Combined with γ = √3 + √5, we solve: subtracting 5·γ = 5√3 + 5√5 from 3√5 + 5√3 gives (γ·√15 − 5γ) = −2√5, so √5 = (5γ − γ√15)/2 ∈ ℚ(γ). Similarly √3 ∈ ℚ(γ)."

The degree of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5})$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is 4, with <Term k="basis">basis</Term> $\{1, \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5}, \sqrt{15}\}$. So the minimal polynomial of $\sqrt{3} + \sqrt{5}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ has degree 4.
