<SlideLayout title="Artin's primitive element theorem">

Number fields can be generated by two or more elements — $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5})$ is a natural example. But it turns out one element always suffices.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Artin's primitive element theorem">
Every number field $K$ is <Term k="isomorphic">isomorphic</Term> to $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ for some algebraic number $\alpha$. Such an $\alpha$ is called a **primitive element** of $K$.
</Callout>

In other words: every number field is **monogenic** — generated over $\mathbb{Q}$ by a single element. The theorem is existential; it tells you a primitive element exists, not which one to pick.

This is somewhat surprising. $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5})$ looks like it needs two generators. The theorem says it doesn't.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Why we care">
The theorem means every number field has the form $\mathbb{Q}[x]/(P(x))$ for an irreducible polynomial $P$. This gives a very concrete handle on number fields: to study them, we just study irreducible polynomials over $\mathbb{Q}$.
</Callout>

</SlideLayout>
