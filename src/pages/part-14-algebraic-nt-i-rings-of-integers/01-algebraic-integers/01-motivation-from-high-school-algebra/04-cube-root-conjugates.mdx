<SlideLayout title="The cube root of 2 has two conjugates">

The minimal polynomial of $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is $x^3 - 2$. This has three roots:

$$\sqrt[3]{2}, \quad \sqrt[3]{2}\,e^{2\pi i/3}, \quad \sqrt[3]{2}\,e^{4\pi i/3}$$

Any polynomial with rational coefficients that vanishes at $\sqrt[3]{2}$ must also vanish at the other two. The three roots travel as a package.

So even though $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is a real number with no obvious "conjugate" over the reals, it does have conjugates over $\mathbb{Q}$ — they happen to be complex.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Uniqueness of the minimal polynomial">
Monicity and minimality together guarantee uniqueness. If $P$ and $P'$ are both monic minimal polynomials of $\alpha$, then $P \mid P'$ and $P' \mid P$ (by the theorem on the previous slide), so $P = P'$.
</Callout>
