<SlideLayout title="Adjoining an algebraic number">

Every algebraic number generates a number field, and the degree of the field equals the degree of the algebraic number.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Structure of ℚ(α)">
Let $\alpha$ be the root of an <Term k="irreducible">irreducible</Term> polynomial $P(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ of degree $m$. Then
$$\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) \cong \mathbb{Q}[x]/(P(x)),$$
and the set $\{1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \dots, \alpha^{m-1}\}$ is a <Term k="basis">basis</Term> of $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ as a $\mathbb{Q}$-<Term k="vectorSpace">vector space</Term>. In particular, $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}] = m = \deg P$.
</Callout>

So $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ has basis $\{1, \sqrt[3]{2}, \sqrt[3]{4}\}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ and degree 3. Every element looks like $a + b\sqrt[3]{2} + c\sqrt[3]{4}$ for $a, b, c \in \mathbb{Q}$.

<Callout kind="remark" title="ℚ(α) vs ℚ[α]">
You might wonder if $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ (field generated by $\alpha$) differs from $\mathbb{Q}[\alpha]$ (ring of polynomials in $\alpha$). For algebraic $\alpha$ they coincide: since $\alpha$ is algebraic, inverses of nonzero elements of $\mathbb{Q}[\alpha]$ are also polynomials in $\alpha$ (by the minimal polynomial relation). So no extra denominators are needed.
</Callout>

</SlideLayout>
