We now step back and define the full class of numbers we're dealing with.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Algebraic number">
An **algebraic number** is any $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ that is the root of some nonzero polynomial with coefficients in <Term k="QQ">$\mathbb{Q}$</Term>. The set of all algebraic numbers is denoted $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="remark">
Equivalently, we can use polynomials with coefficients in <Term k="ZZ">$\mathbb{Z}$</Term> — any rational polynomial can be scaled to an integer polynomial without changing its roots.
</Callout>

Algebraic numbers form a <Term k="field">field</Term> $\overline{\mathbb{Q}} \subset \mathbb{C}$. We'll take this on faith for now (the next chapter gives tools to prove it cleanly).

Numbers that are *not* algebraic are called **transcendental**. Both $\pi$ and $e$ are transcendental — this is a theorem, not obvious. Most real numbers are transcendental (in a precise measure-theoretic sense), even though we rarely encounter them explicitly.

</SlideLayout>
