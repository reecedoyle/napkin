The most important structural fact about the second derivative is that it is symmetric.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Symmetry of D²f">
Let $f \colon U \to W$ with $U \subseteq V$ open.
If $(D^2f)_p$ exists at some $p \in U$, then for any $v_1, v_2 \in V$:
$$
(D^2f)_p(v_1, v_2) = (D^2f)_p(v_2, v_1).
$$
</Callout>

An immediate corollary about mixed partials:

<Callout kind="corollary" title="Clairaut's theorem: mixed partials commute">
Let $f \colon U \to \mathbb{R}$ be twice differentiable.
Then for any basis directions $\mathbf{e}_i, \mathbf{e}_j$ and point $p$:
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{e}_i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{e}_j}(p)
= \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{e}_j}\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{e}_i}(p).
$$
</Callout>

In coordinates: $f_{xy} = f_{yx}$ — the order of partial differentiation does not matter,
provided $f$ is twice differentiable. This is <Term k="clairauts">Clairaut's theorem</Term>.
