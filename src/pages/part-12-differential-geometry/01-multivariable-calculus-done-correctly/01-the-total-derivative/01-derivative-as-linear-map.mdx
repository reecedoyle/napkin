<SlideLayout title="The derivative as a linear map">

High school calculus taught us to define $f'(p)$ as a **number** — the slope of the tangent line.
But think about what that slope really does: it tells us that moving $\varepsilon$ away from $p$
changes $f$ by about $f'(p)\cdot\varepsilon$.

That is exactly what a <Term k="linearMap">linear map</Term> does.

<Callout kind="remark" title="The fundamental idea">
The derivative of $f$ at $p$ approximates $f$ near $p$ by a linear function.
The number $f'(p)$ is only a compressed encoding of that linear map,
valid because every linear map $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is just multiplication by a scalar.
</Callout>

For functions $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ the compression is lossless.
The moment the domain is $\mathbb{R}^n$ for $n \ge 2$, we lose information by reducing to a single slope.
**The morally correct object is always the linear map, not the number.**
