<SlideLayout title="Computing the total derivative in practice">

Now that we have the continuous partials theorem, computing $Df$ is straightforward:
take partial derivatives, check they are continuous, write $Df$ as their linear combination.

<Callout kind="example" title="Total derivative of f(x,y) = x sin y + x²y⁴">
Compute the partial derivatives:
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,y) = \sin y + 2xy^4, \qquad
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x,y) = x\cos y + 4x^2y^3.
$$
Both are continuous everywhere, so by the <Term k="continuousPartials">continuous partials theorem</Term>,
$f$ is differentiable and
$$
(Df)_{(x,y)} = (\sin y + 2xy^4)\,\mathbf{e}_1^\vee + (x\cos y + 4x^2y^3)\,\mathbf{e}_2^\vee.
$$
</Callout>

At the point $(1, 0)$ we get $(Df)_{(1,0)} = 0 \cdot \mathbf{e}_1^\vee + 1 \cdot \mathbf{e}_2^\vee$.
