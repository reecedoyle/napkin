<SlideLayout title="Partial derivatives defined">

Pick a basis $\mathbf{e}_1, \dots, \mathbf{e}_n$ for $V$.
The projection principle gives a dual basis $\mathbf{e}_1^\vee, \dots, \mathbf{e}_n^\vee$ for $V^\vee$,
so we can write

$$
Df = \psi_1 \mathbf{e}_1^\vee + \cdots + \psi_n \mathbf{e}_n^\vee
$$

for some scalar functions $\psi_i \colon U \to \mathbb{R}$.
What are the $\psi_i$?

<Callout kind="definition" title="Partial derivative">
The **$i$-th partial derivative** of $f \colon U \to \mathbb{R}$ is the function
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{e}_i}(p)
\;=\; \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(p + t\mathbf{e}_i) - f(p)}{t}.
$$
It measures how $f$ changes when we move only in the $\mathbf{e}_i$ direction.
</Callout>

One can check: if $(Df)_p$ exists, then $(Df)_p(\mathbf{e}_i) = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{e}_i}(p)$.
So the coordinate functions of $Df$ are exactly the <Term k="partialDerivative">partial derivatives</Term>:

$$
\boxed{Df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{e}_1} \cdot \mathbf{e}_1^\vee + \cdots + \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{e}_n} \cdot \mathbf{e}_n^\vee.}
$$

</SlideLayout>
