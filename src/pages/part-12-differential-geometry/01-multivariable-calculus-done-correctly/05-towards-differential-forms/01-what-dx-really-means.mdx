<SlideLayout title="What dx and dy really mean">

We have reached the punchline of this chapter.
The total derivative $Df$ sends each point $p \in U$ to a linear functional $(Df)_p \in V^\vee$.
For a scalar-valued function $f \colon U \to \mathbb{R}$:

$$
Df \colon U \to V^\vee.
$$

This is precisely what a **differential 1-form** is: a smooth assignment of a covector to each point.

<Callout kind="remark" title="The mystery of dx">
In first-year calculus you wrote $\int f(x)\,dx$ and were told the $dx$ was "an infinitesimal" or "just notation". In fact, $dx$ is the total derivative of the coordinate function $x \colon \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$:
$$
dx = D(x\text{-coordinate}) = \mathbf{e}_1^\vee.
$$
Similarly $dy = \mathbf{e}_2^\vee$, $dz = \mathbf{e}_3^\vee$.
These are the elements of the <Term k="dualBasis">dual basis</Term> — the coordinate covectors.
</Callout>

So when we write $df = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dx + \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,dy$,
we are literally spelling out the decomposition

$$
Df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{e}_1} \cdot \mathbf{e}_1^\vee + \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{e}_2} \cdot \mathbf{e}_2^\vee
$$

in the dual basis $\{dx, dy\}$. The notation was right all along — now we know what it means.

</SlideLayout>
