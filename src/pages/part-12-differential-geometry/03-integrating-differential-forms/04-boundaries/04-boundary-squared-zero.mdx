<SlideLayout title="The boundary of a boundary is zero">

There is a fundamental algebraic fact about the boundary operator:

<Callout kind="theorem" title="∂² = 0">
For any $k$-chain $c$, we have $\partial(\partial c) = 0$.
</Callout>

This mirrors the fact that $d^2 = 0$ for the exterior derivative. In fact, Stokes' theorem will connect these two "squaring to zero" properties directly.

**Geometric intuition.** The boundary of a boundary has no boundary of its own. For a square: its boundary is a closed loop of four edges; the boundary of that loop is $\sum_i \{p_{i+1}\} - \{p_i\}$, which telescopes to zero (each vertex appears once with each sign).

The algebraic proof is "just sigma notation": expanding $\partial(\partial c)$ reveals that each $(k-2)$-face of $c$ appears exactly twice, with opposite signs, from two different $i$-values.

<Callout kind="remark">
The sequence of spaces of $k$-chains linked by the boundary operator $\partial$ forms a **chain complex** — a cornerstone of algebraic topology. The fact that <Term k="boundarySquaredZero">$\partial^2 = 0$</Term> is what makes the notion of "cycles" and "boundaries" well-defined.
</Callout>

</SlideLayout>
