<SlideLayout title="Why Stokes' theorem works">

The proof of <Term k="stokesThm">Stokes' theorem</Term> reduces to the one-dimensional fundamental theorem of calculus, using two key tools:

1. **Pullback commutes with $d$:** $\phi^\ast(d\alpha) = d(\phi^\ast\alpha)$.
2. **Fubini's theorem:** we can integrate one variable at a time.

Concretely: pull $\alpha$ back to the unit box $[0,1]^k$ via $c$. On the box, $c^\ast\alpha$ is a $(k-1)$-form that can be written explicitly. Applying $d$ and using the one-variable fundamental theorem in each coordinate direction produces the boundary terms — which are exactly the faces of $\partial c$.

The alternating signs $(-1)^{i+1}$ in the definition of $\partial c$ are chosen precisely to make the algebra work out: they cancel the signs that appear when we apply the fundamental theorem of calculus to each coordinate direction.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Dual pair: d and ∂">
Stokes' theorem says that $d$ and $\partial$ are **adjoint operators**: integrating $d\alpha$ over $c$ is the same as integrating $\alpha$ over $\partial c$. This is analogous to integration by parts, where $\int f g' = -\int f' g$ (plus boundary terms).
</Callout>
