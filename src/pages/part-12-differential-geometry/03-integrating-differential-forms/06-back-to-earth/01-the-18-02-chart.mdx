Now let's connect what we've built to the vector calculus you saw in a first multivariable calculus course (MIT's 18.02 or equivalent).

Think of a table with rows labeled by the domain ($\RR^1$, $\RR^2$, $\RR^3$) and columns labeled by the dimension of the integral (0-D, 1-D, 2-D, 3-D). Every entry describes some type of integral that appears in first-year calculus. The goal: translate each entry into the differential-forms language.

**What we can explain:**

- The diagonal entries (integrating an $n$-form over an $n$-cell) are straightforward.
- The "purplish" integrals over vector fields (work and flux) correspond to integrating 1-forms and 2-forms.
- Every red "differentiation" arrow in the chart corresponds to the exterior derivative $d$.
- Every arrow gives an instance of Stokes' theorem.

**What we cannot explain:** the "green" line integrals for scalar functions — arc length $\int |c'(t)| dt$ and surface area. These are **not** integrals of differential forms. The integrand $|\cdot|$ involves a norm, which is not alternating, so it cannot be a differential form.

<Callout kind="remark">
Arc length is **not** the integral of any 1-form. This is a theorem, not a gap in our framework — it genuinely lies outside the world of differential forms.
</Callout>
