<SlideLayout title="The angle form — closed but not exact">

The most important example of a closed-but-not-exact form lives on the punctured plane.

<Callout kind="example" title="The angle form">
Let $U = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$. Define the <Term k="angleForm">**angle form**</Term>
$$\alpha = \frac{-y\,dx + x\,dy}{x^2 + y^2}.$$
This 1-form measures the infinitesimal change in the polar angle $\theta$ at each point. Intuitively, "$\alpha = d\theta$."

One can verify directly that $d\alpha = 0$ — the angle form is closed.

However, $\alpha$ is **not exact** on $U$. There is no smooth function $\theta \colon U \to \mathbb{R}$ with $d\theta = \alpha$, because the angle $\theta$ is only well-defined modulo $2\pi$ — it wraps around.
</Callout>

The punchline: if we integrate $\alpha$ around a circle enclosing the origin, we get $2\pi \neq 0$. An exact form would always integrate to zero around any loop (by Stokes' theorem). The non-zero integral witnesses that $\alpha$ cannot be $df$ for a global $f$.

</SlideLayout>
