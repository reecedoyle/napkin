<SlideLayout title="Arc length ds is not a 1-form">

You may have integrated $ds$ in calculus: $\int_c ds$ is the arc length of the curve $c$. We can certainly integrate it, so why isn't $ds$ a differential form?

The definition: $ds_p(v) = \|v\|$. This is **not linear**. Specifically, $ds_p(-v) = \|-v\| = \|v\| = ds_p(v)$, but linearity would require $ds_p(-v) = -ds_p(v)$.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Why not linear?">
A 1-form $\alpha$ must satisfy $\alpha_p(\lambda v) = \lambda\,\alpha_p(v)$ for all scalars $\lambda$, including negative ones. But $\|{-v}\| = \|v\| \neq -\|v\|$ (for $v \neq 0$). So $ds$ fails the linearity test.
</Callout>

You may also see the informal expression $ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$ — the square root is a giveaway that $ds$ is not linear in $dx$ and $dy$.

The correct framework for $ds$ is a **1-density**, not a 1-form. Densities generalize forms by weakening linearity to positive homogeneity: a 1-density $\omega_p$ satisfies $\omega_p(\lambda v) = \lambda\,\omega_p(v)$ only for $\lambda \ge 0$ (scaling a direction up scales the value, but reversing the direction need not flip the sign). A 1-form is the special case that is additionally fully linear.

</SlideLayout>
