<SlideLayout title="Stokes' theorem — boundary of a boundary">

We saw that $\int_c df = f(b) - f(a)$: integrating $df$ over a curve recovers the change at the boundary (the endpoints). This pattern generalizes.

Given a 1-form $\alpha$, its exterior derivative $d\alpha$ is the 2-form that records **how $\alpha$ behaves on the boundary of a small parallelogram**. At a point $p$, for vectors $v$ and $w$:

$(d\alpha)_p(v, w)$ = the integral of $\alpha$ around the boundary of the parallelogram spanned by $v$ and $w$.

When we tile the full surface $c$ with tiny parallelograms and sum $d\alpha$ over all of them, adjacent interior boundary pieces cancel. Only the **outer boundary** of $c$ survives:

$$\int_c d\alpha = \int_{\partial c} \alpha.$$

This is <Term k="stokesTheorem">**Stokes' theorem**</Term>. It will be proved carefully in the next chapter; for now it is the geometric engine behind everything.
