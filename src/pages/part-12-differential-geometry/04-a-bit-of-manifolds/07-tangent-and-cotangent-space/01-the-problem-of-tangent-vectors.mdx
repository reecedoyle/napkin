<SlideLayout title="(Optional) The challenge of tangent vectors">

We used the phrase "tangent space $T_p(M)$" earlier without defining it. For embedded manifolds (those sitting in $\mathbb{R}^N$), the tangent space is just the geometric tangent plane. But smooth manifolds are **intrinsic** objects, not subsets of $\mathbb{R}^N$.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Analogy with groups">
A group $G$ is defined abstractly — not as a subgroup of $S_n$, even though Cayley's theorem says it embeds into one. Similarly, a smooth manifold $M$ is defined without reference to an ambient space.

During the 19th century, a "group" literally meant a subset of $\text{GL}(n)$ or $S_n$. Only later did mathematicians realise the intrinsic definition is more powerful. The same evolution happened with manifolds.
</Callout>

We want a definition of the tangent space that:
1. Depends only on the smooth structure of $M$, not on any ambient space.
2. Agrees with the geometric tangent plane when $M$ happens to be embedded.

The trick: **tangent vectors are partial-derivative-like maps on smooth functions**.

</SlideLayout>
