<SlideLayout title="Picturing orientations — normal vectors">

The formal definition of orientability uses volume forms, but for "embedded manifolds" (those sitting inside some $\mathbb{R}^N$) there is a more geometric picture.

For a smooth hypersurface $M \subset \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$, orientability is equivalent to being able to **continuously pick a unit normal vector** at every point of $M$. Such a choice is called a **normal field**.

For example, on the sphere $S^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3$, we can always point the normal outward (away from the center) — or always inward — and the choice is continuous and consistent everywhere. So $S^2$ is orientable.

The Möbius strip fails: if you travel around the center circle, the chosen normal rotates by $180°$ and ends up pointing the opposite way, so no consistent global choice exists.

<Callout kind="remark" title="Non-rigorous but correct intuition">
We haven't yet defined the <Term k="tangentSpace">tangent space</Term> $T_p(M)$ rigorously (that comes in §7). The normal-field picture is non-rigorous but gives correct intuition for whether a space is orientable.
</Callout>
