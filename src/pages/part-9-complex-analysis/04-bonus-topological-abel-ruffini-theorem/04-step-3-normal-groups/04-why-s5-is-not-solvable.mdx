<SlideLayout title="Why S₅ is not solvable">

The derived series of $S_5$ is:
$$S_5 \supset A_5 \supset A_5 \supset A_5 \supset \cdots$$

Here $[S_5, S_5] = A_5$ (the even permutations — the commutator of two odd permutations is even, and $A_5$ is generated by 3-cycles which are themselves commutators of transpositions). And $[A_5, A_5] = A_5$ because <Term k="A5">$A_5$</Term> is simple and non-abelian.

<Callout kind="remark" title="What 'simple' means here">
$A_5$ is **simple**: its only normal subgroups are $\{e\}$ and $A_5$ itself. Since $[A_5, A_5]$ is a normal subgroup of $A_5$, it must be either $\{e\}$ or $A_5$. If it were $\{e\}$, then $A_5$ would be abelian — but $A_5$ has order 60 and contains non-commuting elements (a 3-cycle and a 5-cycle don't commute). So $[A_5, A_5] = A_5$.
</Callout>

The proof that $A_5$ is simple — and hence that the quintic is not solvable by radicals — is left as the problem for this chapter. It's a calculation: you show $A_5$ has no normal subgroup of order 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, or 30. The key insight is that $A_5$'s five 3-cycle conjugacy classes can't be split without breaking normality.

</SlideLayout>
