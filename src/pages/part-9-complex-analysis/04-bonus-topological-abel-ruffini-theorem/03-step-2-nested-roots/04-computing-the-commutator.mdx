<SlideLayout title="Computing the commutator permutation">

Let's verify that the commutator $(12)(23)(12)^{-1}(23)^{-1}$ in $S_5$ is actually non-trivial.

Since transpositions are their own inverses, $(12)^{-1} = (12)$ and $(23)^{-1} = (23)$. So the commutator is $(12)(23)(12)(23)$.

Let's track what happens to each element:
- Start with element $1$: $(12)$ sends $1 \to 2$; $(23)$ sends $2 \to 3$; $(12)$ sends $3 \to 3$; $(23)$ sends $3 \to 2$. Net: $1 \to 2$.
- Start with $2$: $(12)$ sends $2 \to 1$; $(23)$ sends $1 \to 1$; $(12)$ sends $1 \to 2$; $(23)$ sends $2 \to 3$. Net: $2 \to 3$.
- Start with $3$: $(12)$ sends $3 \to 3$; $(23)$ sends $3 \to 2$; $(12)$ sends $2 \to 1$; $(23)$ sends $1 \to 1$. Net: $3 \to 1$.

So the commutator is the 3-cycle $(132)$ — definitely non-trivial.

<Callout kind="example" title="Result">
The commutator of the transpositions $(12)$ and $(23)$ in $S_5$ is the 3-cycle $(132)$, which is a non-trivial element of <Term k="A5">$A_5$</Term>.
</Callout>
