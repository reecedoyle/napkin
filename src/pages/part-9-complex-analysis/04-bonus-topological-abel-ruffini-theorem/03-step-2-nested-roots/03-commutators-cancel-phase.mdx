<SlideLayout title="Commutators cancel phase">

Here is the crucial move. Consider two root-swapping operations:
- $\alpha$: swap $z_1$ and $z_2$ along arcs, accumulating some phase $\phi_\alpha$ in $\sqrt[n]{R}$.
- $\beta$: swap $z_2$ and $z_3$ along arcs, accumulating phase $\phi_\beta$ in $\sqrt[n]{R}$.

Now form the **commutator** loop: do $\alpha$, then $\beta$, then $\alpha^{-1}$ (undo the first swap), then $\beta^{-1}$ (undo the second swap).

The total phase accumulated is:
$$\phi_\alpha + \phi_\beta + (-\phi_\alpha) + (-\phi_\beta) = 0.$$

The phases cancel! The <Term k="liftingPath">lifting</Term> of $\sqrt[n]{R}$ returns to exactly where it started.

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Commutators fix the phase">
The commutator $\alpha \beta \alpha^{-1} \beta^{-1}$ accumulates zero net phase in any $\sqrt[n]{R}$ — because phases are abelian and so cancel.
</Callout>

But what permutation of the roots does this commutator induce? Swapping $(12)$, then $(23)$, then undoing $(12)$, then undoing $(23)$ gives the permutation $(12)(23)(12)^{-1}(23)^{-1}$ in <Term k="S5">$S_5$</Term>. Since permutations are **non-abelian**, this <Term k="commutatorGroup">commutator</Term> is generally non-trivial!

</SlideLayout>
