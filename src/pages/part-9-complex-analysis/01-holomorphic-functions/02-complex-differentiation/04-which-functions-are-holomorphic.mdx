<SlideLayout title="Checking holomorphicity">

Differentiation rules for <Term k="holomorphic">holomorphic functions</Term> match the real calculus rules: sum rule, product rule, chain rule, quotient rule (away from zeros of the denominator). So in practice we rarely compute limits directly.

<Callout kind="example" title="What IS holomorphic">
- $f(z) = z^3 - 2z + 1$ — polynomial, holomorphic everywhere.
- $f(z) = e^{z^2}$ — composition of holomorphic functions, holomorphic everywhere.
- $f(z) = 1/(z-3)$ — holomorphic on $\CC \setminus \{3\}$.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" title="What is NOT holomorphic">
- $f(z) = \ol z$ — conjugation, not holomorphic anywhere.
- $f(z) = |z|^2 = z\ol z$ — not holomorphic (it involves $\ol z$).
- $f(z) = \Re[z]$ — real part, not holomorphic.
</Callout>

The pattern: any formula that uses only $z$ (and not $\ol z$, $\Re z$, or $|z|$) tends to be holomorphic, because it respects the complex multiplication structure.
