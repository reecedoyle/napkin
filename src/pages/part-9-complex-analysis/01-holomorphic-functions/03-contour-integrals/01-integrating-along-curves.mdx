<SlideLayout title="Integrating along curves">

On the real line we integrate functions over intervals $[a,b]$. The complex plane has a richer structure: we can integrate over arbitrary curves, not just straight segments.

A **<Term k="contour">contour</Term>** is a piecewise smooth path $\alpha \colon [a,b] \to \CC$ — think of it as a curve drawn in the plane.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Contour integral">
Given a <Term k="complexFunction">complex function</Term> $f$ and a contour $\alpha \colon [a,b] \to \CC$, the **<Term k="contourIntegral">contour integral</Term>** is
$$\oint_\alpha f(z) \; dz = \int_a^b f(\alpha(t)) \cdot \alpha'(t) \; dt.$$
</Callout>

This looks like a $u$-substitution from calculus: replace $z$ by $\alpha(t)$ and $dz$ by $\alpha'(t) \, dt$.

A key fact: the value does not depend on how $\alpha$ is parametrised. Three different parametrisations of "go counterclockwise around the unit circle once" all give the same integral.
