<SlideLayout title="What makes ℂ special?">

Viewed as functions $\RR^2 \to \RR^2$, complex-valued functions already let us integrate over curves instead of just line segments. But $\CC$ has more: it is a <Term k="field">field</Term>, so we can multiply and divide complex numbers.

This multiplicative structure is what separates <Term k="complexDifferentiable">complex differentiability</Term> from ordinary two-variable calculus. A function $f \colon U \to \CC$ on an <Term k="openSubset">open subset</Term> $U \subseteq \CC$ is holomorphic only if its derivative is a genuine complex number — not just a 2×2 real matrix.

The two headline theorems flowing from this:
1. **Loop integrals vanish.** For a holomorphic function, the contour integral around any closed loop is zero.
2. **Holomorphic = analytic.** Being once complex-differentiable forces $f$ to equal its Taylor series everywhere on its domain.
