<SlideLayout title="Cauchy's differentiation formula">

The Taylor series argument works for any center $p$, not just $p = 0$. The full theorem:

<Callout kind="theorem" title="Cauchy's differentiation formula">
Let $f \colon U \to \CC$ be <Term k="holomorphic">holomorphic</Term> and let $D$ be a disk centered at $p$ bounded by a circle $\gamma$, with $D \subseteq U$. Then on all of $D$,
$$f(z) = \sum_{k \geq 0} c_k (z-p)^k$$
where
$$c_k = \frac{f^{(k)}(p)}{k!} = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_\gamma \frac{f(w)}{(w-p)^{k+1}} \; dw.$$
In particular, all derivatives $f^{(k)}(p)$ exist and equal $k! \cdot c_k$.
</Callout>

The punchline: **complex differentiable once $\Rightarrow$ equal to Taylor series $\Rightarrow$ infinitely differentiable**.

This has no real analogue: a real function can be once differentiable without being twice differentiable, and can be infinitely differentiable without being equal to its Taylor series.
