<SlideLayout title="The logarithmic derivative">

Given a polynomial $P(x) = (x-a_1)^{e_1}\cdots(x-a_n)^{e_n}$, a classical identity gives:

$$\frac{P'(x)}{P(x)} = \frac{e_1}{x-a_1} + \frac{e_2}{x-a_2} + \cdots + \frac{e_n}{x-a_n}$$

This is the <Term k="logarithmicDerivative">**logarithmic derivative**</Term> $P'/P = \frac{d}{dx}\log P$. It converts zeros into simple poles whose residues are the multiplicities.

<Callout kind="proposition" title="Logarithmic derivative of a meromorphic function">
Let $f \colon U \to \mathbb{C}$ be meromorphic. Then $f'/f$ is also meromorphic, with:
- A simple pole at each **zero** of $f$, with residue equal to the zero's multiplicity.
- A simple pole at each **pole** of $f$, with residue equal to **minus** the pole's order.
</Callout>

**Proof idea:** Near a zero/pole at $p = 0$, write $f(z) = c_k z^k + c_{k+1}z^{k+1} + \cdots$ with $k > 0$ (zero) or $k < 0$ (pole). Then $f'(z)/f(z) = k/z + \text{(holomorphic)}$, giving a simple pole with residue $k$.

Poles of $f$ contribute *negative* residues to $f'/f$. Think of a pole of order $m$ as a "zero of multiplicity $-m$."

</SlideLayout>
